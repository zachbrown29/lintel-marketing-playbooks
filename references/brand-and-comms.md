# Brand, Communications, and PR

How an early-stage B2B software company builds a brand it can run, speaks consistently, handles a crisis (including a layoff), keeps investors and recruits informed, and earns honest press. For founders and first marketers at seed to Series B.

**Owns:** brand foundation (purpose, values, personality, positioning brief), visual identity basics, brand voice, brand measurement, brand campaigns, crisis communication (FW-COMMS-001 owns crisis, including layoffs), investor and board communication, employer brand, earned media and press, and what PR can and cannot do.
**Does not own:** positioning and messaging itself (FW-FOUND-003, FW-FOUND-004, FW-PM-002), the ICP (FW-FOUND-001), analyst relations and category strategy (FW-INFLUENCE-001, FW-EMERGING-005), executive thought leadership content (FW-CONTENT-006), the launch plan (FW-PM-003), agency selection and contracts (FW-OPS-001), AI-answer optimization (FW-SEO-008), attribution (FW-FOUND-006, FW-REVOPS-004), and hiring the marketing team (FW-OPS-004).

**Routing**
| The question sounds like | Go to |
|---|---|
| "Our story sounds different everywhere" | Brand foundation, then Brand voice |
| "Buyers don't understand what we do" | Not here. Messaging and positioning files first |
| "Something went wrong and it is public" / "We are doing layoffs" | Crisis block |
| "Candidates are not applying" | Employer brand block |
| "Should we do PR" | PR block |

**Honest gap:** this skill has no framework for all-hands or day-to-day internal communication, or for executive communication beyond investor and board updates. Say so if asked. What exists on employees is the crisis-time employee message, brand training, and employer brand.

## Brand foundation and positioning brief  (FW-BRAND-001)

**When this matters:** different people describe the company differently (sales calls, website, investor deck, job posts); the sales team is about to grow (five or more reps with no two telling it alike is the strongest signal); a fundraise, acquisition, or new marketing leader is coming; win/loss says buyers trust a comparable competitor more.
**Skip it when:** pre-product-market-fit (draft the brief as a hypothesis only); buyers do not understand what you do (that is messaging, fix it first); pipeline is acutely short; a pivot is underway. Under 10 people with the founder on every call: write the one-page brief anyway (cheap, and the first hire inherits it) and build nothing more.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: a logo and colors from founding, no written foundation, three descriptions of the company across site, LinkedIn, and deck, no owner.
- Tier 2 Building: a full strategy document, brand architecture decided (usually one master brand), basic visual and verbal guidelines, a named owner.
- Tier 3 Operating: guidelines cover every touchpoint, external-facing staff are trained, quarterly reviews, a bi-annual consistency audit with a score.
- Tier 4 Compounding: equity measured against competitors and tied to pipeline and win rate; the gap between intended and perceived brand is managed.

**First build, in order**
1. Run a 2 to 3 hour discovery session with the founders and one or two early employees. Capture answers word for word to six questions: what would be missing if you vanished; a decision that cost you money, a customer, or a hire because it was right; how the company would behave as a person at an industry event (what it would refuse to discuss); what your best customer says about you that no competitor would claim; why you started; what is wrong with your industry.
2. Hold three to five buyer conversations with one question: "When you describe us to a colleague who has never heard of us, what do you say?" Keep their exact words.
3. Write a one-page brief (2 to 3 hours): purpose in one sentence (if a competitor could say it, it is not done); three to five values, each with a behavior ("we share our mistakes publicly before anyone asks" beats "transparency"); three to five personality traits as pairs ("direct but never harsh"); positioning (audience in ICP language, category, one credible differentiator).
4. Ask three leaders to describe the company in their own words. If answers diverge, it is not internalized.
5. Name an owner and fix the three highest-frequency touchpoints: website headline, email signature, LinkedIn description.
6. At Series A or later, expand to the strategy document (15 to 25 pages) and keep the story in two forms: 500 to 1,000 words and three sentences. Host guidelines where people work (Notion, organized by "I am writing a prospect email"), not a PDF. Add a 30 to 60 minute onboarding session and a sub-24-hour review for sales decks, job posts, and press releases.

**Decision rules**
- If a rebrand is on the table, ask four things: has the ICP changed, has a competitor taken your position, is the brand costing deals, have you outgrown what it says? None means the rebrand is cosmetic. Any means start with strategy review, not design.
- If past refreshes did not move any brand metric, the foundation never changed. Fix purpose, personality, and positioning before spending on expression.
- If guidelines exist but consistency is under 70%, the cause is access, training, or a skipped review step. Fix those, not the guidelines.
- If leadership alignment is under 60 to 70%, run a 90 minute session where leaders state the brand in their own words. Do not re-present the document.
- If stated values conflict with company behavior, decide whether it is a behavior failure (change the behavior) or a communication failure (explain the decision in terms of the value). The first is the most damaging brand problem.
- If you are PLG, product copy and onboarding tone come before external brand work.
- If you are a solo marketer, do two things only: the brief, and consistency of website, email, and sales materials.
- Hire a brand manager only when a foundation exists to inherit and you have roughly 100 or more employees. Before that, a fractional strategist (2 to 3 days a week for 3 to 6 months) fits Series A.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| One-page brief | 6 to 8 hours total, 3 to 5 buyer conversations | Lintel guidance |
| Brand consistency score | Under 60% weak, 60 to 80% improving, above 80% functional, above 90% strong | Lintel guidance. 100% is unrealistic |
| Leadership alignment | 80%+ at Tier 2. Under 60% means not internalized | Lintel guidance |
| Brand-sourced inbound pipeline | 10 to 20% at Tier 3 | Lintel guidance. Needs a "how did you hear about us" field |
| Brand build budget | Seed $0 to $5,000. Series A $5,000 to $25,000. Series B $20,000 to $80,000 plus $15,000 to $40,000 a year. Series C+ $50,000 to $200,000 if a rebrand is warranted | Lintel guidance |
| Brand share of marketing budget | 5 to 10% early, 10 to 20% growth, 15 to 25% scale | Lintel guidance |

Awareness, consideration, and NPS benchmarks live in the Brand measurement block.

**Where it goes wrong:**
- The strategy is built for the leadership team and the buyer feels nothing, because no buyer input went in.
- Values are "customer focus, innovation, integrity," which every competitor also claims. Test each: would a competitor use it?
- The guidelines are a polished PDF nobody opens, and the personality never reaches the website copy or sales emails.
- A 20-person company builds a 100-page brand bible while nobody in the market knows it exists.

## Visual identity and design system  (FW-BRAND-002)

**When this matters:** website, deck, social, and event materials look like four companies; a funding or enterprise moment will draw scrutiny; the identity says "startup" and you now sell to enterprise (or the reverse); a competitor looks uncomfortably similar.
**Skip it when:** no brand brief exists; pre-product-market-fit; the identity is consistent and recognized (changing it for internal boredom throws away earned recognition). "We need a new logo" is usually wrong: the logo is fine and is used inconsistently with approximated colors and default fonts. Audit use first.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: logo files scattered, colors approximated, default fonts in decks.
- Tier 2 Building: a 10 to 30 page guide (logo, color, type), an asset folder, templates for slides, email headers, and social.
- Tier 3 Operating: a Figma component library, non-designer templates built from it, a brand asset platform, a bi-annual audit.
- Tier 4 Compounding: marketing and product design systems share foundations; visual recognition is measured.

**First build, in order** (a 3 to 4 hour starter kit)
1. Collect every logo version and find a vector master (SVG). A JPEG master degrades at large sizes. Make a Drive folder: Logos, Colors, Fonts, Templates.
2. Pull exact hex codes from the live site. Record hex, RGB, and CMYK (print vendors guess without CMYK). Test every text-on-background pair at WCAG AA, a 4.5:1 contrast ratio for normal text.
3. Specify type per tool (web, slides, docs, email). Name a substitute when the brand font is missing in slides, never "whatever is default." Prefer free Google Fonts so non-designers can comply.
4. Make the logo set: SVG, transparent PNG, PNG on white, reversed PNG. Use one naming pattern.
5. Send the folder and a one-page color and type sheet to everyone who produces external content. Name an owner.
6. Next, build a template for the tool that makes the most external content (usually slides, about 4 hours). At Series A to B add the full spec: one primary color, two to three secondaries, three to five grays, semantic colors kept distinct from brand blue; a two-font system; a logo guide with clear space, minimum size, and 8 to 12 misuse examples. Brief any designer with personality translated into visual terms, five to seven competitor identities, and non-negotiables.

**Decision rules**
- If a color pair fails contrast, fix it within five business days at any maturity. Adjust the color or the text color and record the compliant version.
- If your primary color matches the five nearest competitors (often all blue), raise it as a strategy question before the next design spend.
- If a solo marketer has no design skill, pay a freelancer once ($500 to $2,000) to set up a Canva Brand Kit and 8 to 12 templates. If budget is minimal, a freelancer for the logo set and color sheet runs $50 to $200.
- If sellers build decks from scratch, give them a template for their tool. Each deck otherwise costs 30 to 60 minutes of design decisions.
- If you roll out a rebrand, phase it: weeks 1 to 2 website, email signatures, deck template, LinkedIn. Months 1 to 3 everything else. Otherwise old and new brands mix for months.
- If consistency is under 75%, fix templates and training. Do not commission a refresh.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Visual consistency | Under 60% weak, 60 to 79% improving, 80 to 89% strong, 90%+ excellent | Lintel guidance |
| Digital color and contrast compliance | 100% | WCAG AA is the published standard |
| Non-designer template use | Above 70% good, under 40% means templates do not fit the work | Lintel guidance |
| Budget | Seed $0 to $3,000 only if the logo is not production-ready. Series A $5,000 to $20,000. Series B $15,000 to $50,000 a year plus $10,000 to $20,000 for a design system build | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:**
- A Figma design system is built before anyone has a usable template.
- Brand assets live on one person's laptop.
- The logo is refreshed and treated as the brand project while colors, type, and photos stay inconsistent.
- The palette fails accessibility from the day it ships.

## Brand voice and messaging expression  (FW-BRAND-003)

**When this matters:** several writers, agencies, or AI tools sound like different companies; content is generic enough that a competitor could have published it; you are about to scale content with AI (unguided AI writing spreads sameness faster).
**Skip it when:** brand and messaging are unstable; you publish under about ten pieces a month and the founder edits all of them (the founder's edit is the voice); buyers say you are "confusing" or "vague" (messaging problem, go to FW-FOUND-004 and FW-PM-002); content is weak regardless of writer.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: each writer sounds different; the founder's writing is the only reference.
- Tier 2 Building: a voice guide with dimensions, principles, a vocabulary list, and guidance for the three to five most frequent writing situations.
- Tier 3 Operating: all situations covered, an editorial review, trained new writers, a quarterly scored review.
- Tier 4 Compounding: voice is measured in published work and in how buyers describe you.

**First build, in order** (a 4 to 6 hour one-page voice brief)
1. Collect 10 to 15 of your best external pieces. Pick work the team is proud of. The voice already lives there.
2. Read for energy, confidence (strong claims or hedges), warmth (formal or personal), specificity, and the one thing that would stand out next to ten competitor posts.
3. Write three to five dimensions as pairs ("confident but not arrogant"). The second half stops the trait tipping into its bad extreme.
4. For each, add one do and one don't example. The examples are the brief.
5. Have two writers who did not build it write a LinkedIn post and an outbound email from it. If it does not sound like you, fix the misread dimension's examples.
6. Share it with sales and support, not just marketing.
7. Next, the full guide: a one-page intro written in the voice, dimensions, 6 to 12 principles with before and after, a vocabulary list (avoid with replacements, use, product and category terms), and a tone guide for 6 to 10 situations. Add per-channel rules: homepage (name the outcome), blog (evidence for every claim), LinkedIn (a specific point of view), outbound email (subject under 50 characters, first line about the recipient, one small ask, under 100 words; targeting and compliance live in the sales files).
8. Add an AI appendix: a five to seven sentence voice prompt. Test 10 outputs with it against 10 without and tune until the gap is obvious (about 2 hours).

**Decision rules**
- If a founder says "it doesn't sound right but I can't say why," have them pick the best three and worst three pieces and describe the difference. That is the voice.
- If a guide exists and nobody uses it, build a one-page quick reference (ten points, one example each) and pin it where writers work.
- If outbound reply rates are low, compare five emails from your best and worst reps first. Different writing is a voice fix. Different targeting is outside this file.
- If the voice guide and messaging document disagree on wording, the voice guide wins word choice and the messaging document wins the strategic claim.
- If you use freelancers or agencies, require a guide review and a rewrite exercise before the first paid piece.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Voice consistency (15 to 20 pieces, five dimensions, scored 1 to 5) | Under 60% weak, 60 to 75% improving, 75 to 85% functional, above 85% strong | Lintel guidance. Review quarterly |
| New writer, first two weeks | Above 65%. Under 50% means onboarding is not working | Lintel guidance |
| Banned-word appearance | Under 15% of pieces. Above 30% means the list is ignored | Lintel guidance |
| Best-to-worst function gap | Under 20 points at Tier 3. Above 30 means other teams are untrained | Lintel guidance |
| Editorial review turnaround | 48 hours. Above 72 is a bottleneck | Lintel guidance |
| Budget | Seed $0. Series A $0 to $3,000. Series B $2,000 to $8,000 a year | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:**
- Guidance is abstract ("be human") instead of behavioral ("write the most important sentence first").
- The voice is invented instead of extracted from the best existing writing.
- The guide goes to marketing only, so sales and support keep their own voices.
- The personality is never translated per content type. "Bold" in a headline is not "bold" in an email.

## Brand measurement and equity  (FW-BRAND-004)

**When this matters:** you are spending on brand and cannot tell if anything moved; execs argue brand versus performance with no data; a rebrand, big campaign, or fundraise needs a before-picture; win/loss shows buyers pick or avoid you on reputation.
**Skip it when:** no brand strategy exists; you are under about 50 employees and the constraint is basic awareness; the ICP is unconfirmed. At seed, ask "how did you hear about us" at the end of every first meeting for 20 to 30 conversations. The spread of answers shows your main awareness channel for free.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: no data; perception comes from anecdote.
- Tier 2 Building: a quarterly survey (unaided and aided awareness, consideration, two to three attributes, NPS), one competitor benchmarked, two quarters of trend.
- Tier 3 Operating: all five equity dimensions (awareness, associations, perceived quality, loyalty, proprietary assets), two to three competitors, a modeled score, a documented link to commercial trends, reported to the board.
- Tier 4 Compounding: an annual rigorous study, brand data drives investment decisions beyond marketing.

**First build, in order** (a 4 to 6 hour pulse survey)
1. Survey your ICP, not your customer list: 60 to 70% non-customers. Source from LinkedIn invitations, industry communities, or conference lists for free, or buy panel completes (about $3 to $8 each for B2B).
2. Write ten questions. Ask unaided awareness first, before your name appears anywhere, then aided awareness, consideration, attribute ratings, NPS, and first-choice preference. Randomize competitor order, use one scale, screen out non-ICP respondents up front.
3. Collect 50 minimum, 100 for steadier direction, over two to four weeks. A $10 to $15 gift card or a promise to share results lifts response.
4. Log the baseline in a Google Sheet, one row per wave. Treat wave one as a baseline. A trend needs two or more waves.
5. At Tier 2, move to a 15 to 20 question quarterly survey (about 7 minutes). Keep panel criteria constant. Outsource only the annual comprehensive study.

**Decision rules**
- If unaided awareness is under 10% at Series B or later, awareness is the constraint. Put brand money on reach and frequency first.
- If aided awareness beats consideration by more than 20 points, buyers know you and do not trust you. That is a proof gap: case studies with outcome numbers, references, independent validation. Cut awareness spend.
- If the survey is strong but win/loss shows weak perception, the panel is too friendly. If customers exceed 40% of respondents, rebalance to 25 to 30%. Trust win/loss.
- If your score rose but a competitor's rose faster, report both. Relative position fell.
- If a CFO challenges brand spend, offer one survey of 100 buyers (about $1,500, four weeks) and a spend proposal sized to the gaps. Later, after about six quarters, compare awareness trend to inbound pipeline trend on a dual-axis chart. A correlation of 0.5 to 0.65 supports a qualitative case. Say plainly that it is correlation, not proof.
- If review-site ratings and perceived-quality scores fall together, the problem is product and customer experience. Escalate to product.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Unaided awareness | Under 10% early, 10 to 25% growing, 25 to 50% strong, above 50% leader | Lintel guidance |
| Aided awareness | About 2 to 3 times unaided | Lintel guidance |
| Consideration (of aware buyers) | 40 to 60% at Tier 2. Under 30% means known but not trusted | Lintel guidance |
| Attribute scores | Under 3.5 out of 5 on an attribute you want to own means the association is not built | Lintel guidance. Trend and competitor gap matter more |
| Pulse cost | $250 to $500 minimum (50 respondents). $750 to $1,500 for 50 to 75 with gift cards | Lintel guidance |
| Annual budget | Series A $2,000 to $6,000. Series B $12,000 to $35,000. Series C+ $30,000 to $80,000 | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:**
- Surveying only customers, which inflates every score.
- Asking unaided awareness after naming the company, which ruins the most important number.
- Running one wave and calling it an answer.
- A 30-question survey that busy B2B respondents abandon.

## Brand campaigns and awareness  (FW-BRAND-005)

**When this matters:** unaided awareness is under about 15% and you are left off first shortlists; a competitor is building awareness faster; a launch, funding round, or major customer makes attention cheap.
**Skip it when:** brand strategy is unstable; visual and voice starter kits are missing; no baseline survey exists (you cannot show what the campaign did); pipeline is acutely short (demand generation first).

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: nearly all spend is demand generation; brand impressions are accidental.
- Tier 2 Building: a brief per campaign, one a quarter, a protected line item, results compared with the survey.
- Tier 3 Operating: campaigns chosen to close the specific survey gaps; paid, earned, and owned media coordinated.
- Tier 4 Compounding: each campaign has a forecast brand-health impact and a post-campaign review that feeds the next brief.

**First build, in order** (6 to 8 hours to plan the first campaign)
1. Pick the gap from the baseline: unaided under 10% means awareness. Unaided 10 to 20% with a consideration gap over 20 points means consideration (proof). Unaided over 20% with attribute scores under 3.5 out of 5 means association-building.
2. Write a one-page brief: the objective with current and target ("raise unaided awareness among [ICP] from 9% to 16% over two quarters"), audience, which brand-foundation elements it expresses, one sentence for the idea (often the founding insight), channel plan, measurement plan, success definition.
3. Use LinkedIn sponsored content. Aim at 3,000 to 10,000 ICP-matched buyers. At Series A, about $2,000 to $4,000 a quarter at 4,000 to 6,000 people fits.
4. Keep it clean: no conversion goal, landing page, gate, or lead form. A conversion goal changes who the platform shows it to and what you measure.
5. Make three to five creative variants from the brand kit (one distinctive visual, a one-sentence headline, two to three sentences, no call to action). Test two or three in week one.
6. Watch reach, frequency, and engagement weekly. 0.5 to 1% engagement suggests resonance. Optimize for frequency to the ICP, not clicks.
7. Re-run the survey after and compare with baseline. That comparison is next year's investment case.

**Decision rules**
- If you are seed stage, the founder is the campaign: one conference talk a quarter where the ICP is the audience, community participation, LinkedIn under their own name. Budget 3 to 5 founder hours a week, no paid media.
- If you are a solo marketer, run one quarterly awareness campaign, boost the founder's two to three best posts a month ($200 to $500), and publish one annual research piece (about 10 pages). About $3,000 to $5,000 a quarter.
- If two quarters show no awareness lift, check frequency (average under 5 means the audience is too big for the budget), audience quality (does reach match the ICP), and distinctiveness (show five ICP buyers the creative without a logo and ask who it is from).
- If the brand budget is threatened by a pipeline dip, do not fold it into demand gen. Show whether brand-exposed buyers convert better, note that cuts hit results 6 to 12 months out, and offer a compromise: switch the quarter from awareness to consideration.
- If the CFO asks for ROI, use the argument the library credits to LinkedIn's B2B Institute ("The 5% Problem," 2019): only about 5% of your market is in an active buying process at any time. Demand generation reaches them. Brand reaches the rest early. Then show your awareness and consideration gap.
- If you are PLG, community and word of mouth beat paid brand media. If you sell enterprise, the strongest moves are analyst relations (FW-INFLUENCE-001), executive speaking, and press your buying personas read.
- If you are at Series B or later, add an annual category research report ($10,000 to $30,000). It becomes the reference others cite.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| First campaign audience | 3,000 to 10,000 ICP buyers. Average frequency should reach 5+ | Lintel guidance |
| Same $3,000 at two audience sizes | 50,000 people means about one impression each. 5,000 people means about ten | Lintel guidance, illustrative |
| Annual brand campaign budget | Series A $8,000 to $18,000. Series B $30,000 to $100,000. Series C+ $150,000 to $500,000+ | Lintel guidance |
| Share of marketing budget | Series B about 10 to 25% | The two Lintel sources say 10 to 20% and 15 to 25% |

**Where it goes wrong:**
- A conversion goal is added "since we want leads too."
- The audience is too big for the budget, so nobody remembers the message.
- The creative looks like every competitor's: same stock photos, same colors, same aspirational slogans.
- Brand money is pooled with demand gen and drained whenever pipeline dips.

## Crisis communication, including layoffs  (FW-COMMS-001)

**When this matters:** a breach, serious outage, bad leadership exit, regulatory action, bad press, or viral post has hit before with no plan; a fundraise, acquisition, or large enterprise deal is coming; you sell to enterprises, public companies, or government; you operate in a regulated field with legal disclosure deadlines; you are planning a layoff.
**Skip it when:** pre-product and pre-revenue; no customers, employees, or press relationships yet; stealth with no public presence.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: no plan, spokesperson, or drafted statement; the CEO writes live and legal gets no context.
- Tier 2 Building: a written plan, response team list, three or more pre-drafted holding statements, a first-60-minutes checklist, counsel review.
- Tier 3 Operating: annual plan review, a tabletop exercise in the last 12 months, a trained spokesperson, monitoring, templates for eight or more scenarios.
- Tier 4 Compounding: tied to risk management, PR counsel on retainer, a board protocol, comms in security and product risk reviews.

**First build, in order** (4 to 8 hours total)
1. Name your top three crisis scenarios in one sentence each (1 to 2 hours). Typical: a major outage or data loss, a security incident, a leadership or personnel crisis. Add regulatory action if you are regulated, a viral attack if you are visible.
2. List the response team (30 minutes): CEO, legal counsel (external if none in-house), comms lead, customer success, technical lead. Mobile numbers and a backup for each, stored where anyone can open it.
3. Write the first-60-minutes checklist (below), one page, numbered. People do not read narratives in a crisis.
4. Draft one holding statement per scenario (1 to 2 hours): 2 to 4 sentences saying you know, you are working on it, you apologize if customers are affected, and where the next update will appear. It never guesses at cause, assigns blame, or states anything unverified. Counsel reviews each (20 to 30 minutes).
5. Name a primary and backup spokesperson. "No comment" reads as guilt. "We are investigating and will update you" works better.
6. At Tier 2 and up: a stakeholder map, a template set per scenario (holding, full statement, customer email, employee message, social), a post-incident review process, an annual tabletop, Google Alerts at minimum, and a social media protocol.

**The first 60 minutes**
1. Phone the CEO and legal counsel (not Slack or email).
2. Get the team on a call within 30 minutes.
3. Assign one person to establish facts. Say nothing outside until minimum facts are verified. A statement you must correct within 24 hours does more damage than silence.
4. Pull the holding statement for this crisis type.
5. Pause all scheduled marketing and social posts. One named person must be able to do this within 15 minutes.
6. Decide who approves the first statement.
7. Issue the holding statement within 60 minutes if the event is or will be public.

**Triage first.** An incident stays inside operations. An issue has outside impact and needs direct customer contact and a prepared statement. A crisis threatens reputation, customer relationships, continuity, or legal standing and needs the full protocol. One negative post from a non-customer is an incident. Treating it as a crisis wastes leadership time and can escalate it.

**Five crisis types**
| Type | Defining feature | Posture |
|---|---|---|
| Data or security incident | Legal deadlines. Regulators and affected customers hear before press | Factual and owning: found, contained, notifying, what we know |
| Product or operational failure | Recovery runs alongside comms | Transparent. Updates must match engineering's real timeline |
| Leadership or personnel crisis | Board, investors, and employees hear before media | Human voice, first person, specific, warm |
| Regulatory or legal action | Counsel approves every statement | Measured. No admissions or outcome guessing |
| Reputational or social | Speed set by amplification | True accusation: own it. False: correct with evidence. Respond once, factually |

**Choosing posture** uses Situational Crisis Communication Theory (W. Timothy Coombs): match the response to how responsible the public thinks you are. False accusation you could not prevent: correct it with evidence. Unforeseeable event handled in good faith: acknowledge the impact without full blame. You knew the risk and did nothing, or it came from a harmful choice: only a full apology with specific ownership and fair compensation restores trust.

**Who hears, in order**
1. CEO and legal counsel, immediately.
2. Board chair within two hours of confirming a material incident. Full board and lead investors within 24 hours. Inform them, do not ask permission.
3. Directly affected customers, before any public statement. For your top 20 accounts, a personal call from the CEO or head of sales before the email. Others get an email from a named executive.
4. All employees, in the same window as customers and not after the public statement. Give them what happened, what you are doing, the official statement, and what to say if asked.
5. Regulators, per the legal deadline. In EU data incidents the regulator comes before customers.
6. Press and public.

The most common violation is going to press before affected customers know. That turns a crisis into a betrayal.

**Layoffs (this file owns the announcement).** The library has no layoff script. Below is the general sequence applied, not extra invented steps. Get employment counsel first on notice and severance rules.
1. Settle facts and message with the CEO, counsel, and comms lead before anyone else hears.
2. Treat it as a material event for the board and lead investors (chair within two hours, full board and leads within 24 hours; an inference from the material-event rule). FW-COMMS-002 sets routine investor wording.
3. Tell employees in the same window as, or before, any public statement. Give them the facts, the statement, and what to say to customers and candidates.
4. Reach any affected customers directly before the public statement.
5. Use the CEO's first-person human voice: plain and specific. Never "the company wishes to address concerns."
6. Pause scheduled promotional posts. Resume 48 to 72 hours after things settle.
7. Route press only through the spokesperson.
8. Hand recovery to FW-COMMS-003. Before employer brand posts or hiring pushes, fix the internal reality: current employees must be able to speak honestly and positively about direction, or candidates will hear otherwise in interviews. State that the layoff happened and give the context that makes the opportunity worth taking.

**Decision rules**
- If legal wants silence and comms wants a statement, the CEO decides. A factual holding statement that does not guess or admit liability usually satisfies both. Total silence is rarely right outside active litigation.
- If the crisis is a breach, leadership scandal, or company-wide failure, use a first-person CEO statement. Keep corporate "we" for status pages and engineering reports.
- If EU personal data is involved, GDPR requires notice to the lead supervisory authority within 72 hours of discovery and to affected people without undue delay when risk is high. That is the law, not Lintel guidance. Call counsel at once. In the US, state breach laws differ, so have counsel build a state matrix.
- If you expect sustained press and have no firm, decide within two hours whether to bring in a PR firm with crisis experience. After the first coverage runs, they manage a story rather than shape one.
- If the crisis is an outage, update at least every four hours in business hours and every eight outside them until resolved. A 12 to 18 hour silence reads as "it is worse than they said."
- If the CEO wants to post their own take mid-crisis, no. Everyone goes through the comms lead.
- If someone wants to promise "a full update in two hours," don't. Commit only to a time you control.
- After resolution, send a resolution statement (what was fixed, what prevents recurrence), follow up directly with affected customers on any remedy, and wait 48 to 72 hours before normal promotion.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Time to first outside message | Under 60 minutes for a broad public outage. Under four hours where facts must be gathered. Over four is poor | Lintel guidance |
| Time to customer notification | Under two hours for immediate impact. Under 24 for delayed impact | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:**
- An apology with no explanation or action because legal stripped the substance. Counsel and comms write it together.
- Corporate language in a people crisis.
- Scheduled promotional posts keep running during an incident.
- Press hears first, customers second, employees third. The reverse protects trust.

## Investor and board communication  (FW-COMMS-002)

**When this matters:** you closed a seed or Series A with institutional investors or board members who get no structured updates; a raise is within 12 months and the narrative is inconsistent; a big event (lost customer, miss, pivot, executive exit) happened and investors have not heard; board prep eats leadership time.
**Skip it when:** pre-seed with friends and family only (a quarterly CEO note is enough); no product-market-fit signal yet; runway under six months (survival first, though a short honest note still beats silence).

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: investors hear when they ask; board decks are rebuilt each time; the data room is stale.
- Tier 2 Building: a monthly update on a fixed day, board materials 72 hours ahead, defined metrics, a material-event rule.
- Tier 3 Operating: tailored updates, quarterly one-on-ones with leads, a CEO letter with every board pack, a standing fundraise-ready state.
- Tier 4 Compounding: a CFO or IR owner, a living data room, pre-IPO readiness review where relevant.

**First build, in order** (8 to 12 hours across month one)
1. Send the first update now. Include one line acknowledging any gap ("I want to re-establish a regular update rhythm"), ARR or MRR with month and year growth, two to three specific wins, one or two honest lowlights with the response, runway in months, and one specific ask. Five-minute read, narrative under 500 words, metrics on top. First one takes 2 to 3 hours, later ones 45 to 60 minutes.
2. Before the second update, write one page defining ARR, MRR, net revenue retention, CAC, runway, and headcount. If a definition changes, say so and restate the prior period.
3. Make the update a template and send it on the same day every month, good news or bad.
4. Send board materials 72 hours ahead. Run the meeting as 10 minutes of summary and questions, 20 on one strategic topic, 20 on a specific ask of the board, 10 open.
5. Keep a one-sheet investor list: name, firm, amount and round, board status, contact, and one line on expertise. Use it to aim asks.
6. At the standard build, add a data room (financials, cap table, customer metrics, legal, team, strategy), a one to two page CEO-voice investor narrative, a fixed board deck structure, and a quarterly 30 minute one-on-one with each lead.

**Decision rules**
- If a material event is confirmed (a customer over 5% of ARR is lost, ARR growth runs over 20% under plan, runway falls under nine months, a key executive leaves, a significant product incident, regulatory action, a pivot), tell investors within 24 to 48 hours, before the next update. If it is also a crisis, use the faster FW-COMMS-001 timing.
- If the news is bad, lead with facts: what happened, what you are doing, the updated outlook. Investors who hear it from the CEO first trust the CEO more.
- If you need a down round or bridge, call every lead investor first, at least two hours before the written note.
- If a raise is within 12 months and no maintained data room exists, build it before any investor conversation. Metric definitions, data room, and narrative take two to three weeks.
- If strategy changed since the last raise, update the narrative and send it to existing investors before meeting new ones. New investors will call the old ones.
- If the cadence lapsed, send the update now with a one-line acknowledgment and no long apology.
- If board members do not read the pre-read, shrink it to a one-page summary and rebuild later. If investors ignore asks, aim each at that investor's network.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Update cadence | Monthly, same day, 100% on time | Lintel guidance |
| Board pre-read lead time | 72 hours minimum, 120 hours target at Tier 3+ | Lintel guidance |
| Board time on strategy | 50%+ at Tier 2, 70%+ at Tier 3+ | Lintel guidance |
| Data room freshness | Financials at most 45 days old. Cap table updated within 30 days of any change | Lintel guidance |
| ARR across update, deck, and data room | Agree within 1% | Lintel guidance |
| Asks that get a useful reply in 30 days | 30 to 50% | Lintel guidance |
| CEO time | About 4 hours a month at seed, 6 to 8 at Series A. Board prep under 8 hours | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:**
- Updates go out only when things are good, so silence teaches investors to fear the worst.
- Metrics are calculated differently from one document to the next.
- No ask, so the investor network goes unused.
- The board hears about a miss for the first time in the meeting.

## Employer brand and talent marketing  (FW-COMMS-003)

**When this matters:** you are hiring for several roles and good candidates decline or cannot tell what you are like; senior roles take over 60 days because too few qualified people apply; review-site ratings are low or repeat a theme; agencies fill most hires at a cost you cannot sustain.
**Skip it when:** under 15 employees and founders fill roles from their networks; a hiring freeze (content with no open roles loses credibility); the product brand does not exist yet; the review-site rating is under 3.5 (fix the cause first or the content reads as spin).

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: no value proposition, a placeholder careers page, generic job descriptions, agencies fill most hires.
- Tier 2 Building: a defined employee value proposition (EVP) used everywhere, a real careers page, impact-led job descriptions, two to four LinkedIn posts a month, a working referral program.
- Tier 3 Operating: a standing content program and owner, a talent community, a measured hiring funnel, referrals at 35%+.
- Tier 4 Compounding: inbound is 50%+ of qualified applications and senior-candidate relationships start 12 to 18 months before roles open.

**First build, in order**
1. Define the EVP by listening (3 to 5 hours over a week). Interview five to eight employees with over six months' tenure (at least two hired in the last year) and two or three candidates who turned you down. Ask why they stay, what they would tell a talented friend, and what almost stopped them or surprised them. Write two to three sentences from what repeats and test them with each person. Never copy another company's EVP.
2. Rewrite job descriptions (30 to 45 minutes each): why the role matters now, three to five outcome-based duties, five to seven real requirements split into must-have and nice-to-have, what you offer in EVP terms, one identical company paragraph.
3. Rebuild the careers page (4 to 8 hours plus design): EVP hero with a real employee photo, who you are, honest specifics ("engineers own features end to end, no handoff to QA because there is no QA team"), the team, practical details (pay approach, equity, benefits, flexibility, location), open roles.
4. Turn on referrals: a weekly "we're hiring" message, a one-step submission, a $3,000 to $7,500 bonus for senior roles paid within 30 days after the hire's 90-day mark, and a reply to every referrer within a week about what happened. Skipping that last step kills referrals.
5. Claim the review-site profile and respond to every review, positive and negative, in a human, non-defensive voice. Invite all current employees to review honestly. Do not hand-pick happy employees, reward reviews, or stagger them to look organic (FTC rules on fake and incentivized reviews, and site policies, apply). One library step suggested otherwise. It is dropped.
6. Then a content calendar (two to four posts a month whether or not roles are open), an interview-experience audit, a candidate survey after every process, and a talent community.

**Decision rules**
- If a role has been open over 45 days with few qualified applicants, decide whether the problem is visibility (more distribution) or relevance (rewrite with EVP language and a stronger opportunity frame).
- If referrals are under 15% of hires, it is one of three things: employees rarely hear about roles, the form has too many steps, or bonuses pay late.
- If offer acceptance falls under about 75%, have a recruiter debrief every decliner by phone. Separate pay (raise ranges earlier) from brand (what in the process created doubt).
- If feedback speed scores badly, commit to 72 hours from interview to feedback, with one person chasing at 48. Candidates with options decide while they wait.
- If employee NPS for the employer is negative, stop. It is a retention and culture problem that brand work will not fix.
- If most candidates never heard of you, put the CEO and leaders on LinkedIn, use investor and advisor introductions (the fastest route to senior people), and create one or two high-visibility pieces (a shared technical post, a talk, a press feature).
- If a stronger competitor keeps winning offers, lead with what you offer that they cannot: mission clarity, scope, impact. For VP and above, use direct outreach from the CEO or board.
- Add a talent marketing manager only when hiring costs pass about $400,000 a year including agency fees.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Employer review rating | 4.0+ before senior candidates read it as a warning. 4.3+ when competing for senior talent | Lintel guidance |
| Referral share of hires | 25 to 35% at Tier 2. 35 to 45% at Tier 3+ | Lintel guidance |
| Agency share of hires | Under 30% at Series B. Under 15% at Series C+ | Lintel guidance |
| Time to fill | Individual contributors 30 to 45 days, senior 45 to 60, VP and above 60 to 90 | Lintel guidance |
| Candidate survey | 4.0+ out of 5. Under 3.8 on any dimension is a fix trigger | Lintel guidance |
| Cost per hire | Referral $2,000 to $5,000. Organic $500 to $2,000. Agency $15,000 to $35,000 for senior | Lintel guidance, rough |
| Budget | Series A $10,000 to $25,000 a year. Series B $40,000 to $100,000 | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:**
- The EVP is copied from another company instead of heard from your own people.
- Long requirement lists scare off qualified candidates. Keep to the five to seven that predict success.
- Referral bonuses pay six months late, so referrals stop.
- Hiring content runs while internal reality contradicts it.

## Press, earned media, and what PR can and cannot do  (FW-PR-001)

**When this matters:** prospects say "never heard of you" or you lose to a better-known competitor; a real news event is 60 to 120 days out (a priced round, a major launch, a named enterprise customer who agrees to go public, original data); enterprise buyers ask for third-party validation in security or procurement reviews; AI assistants name your competitors and not you; a leader has a defensible point of view and 2 to 4 hours a month.
**Skip it when:** you cannot say in one sentence who you serve and why you differ (coverage amplifies a muddy story); product-market fit is in doubt; no news, data, or quotable person for six months; sales cannot take the attention (no clear site, no way to book a demo); demos happen but deals do not close; counsel limits public statements.

**What PR can and cannot do**
| PR can | PR cannot |
|---|---|
| Build trust before sales calls, which warms every other channel | Be judged as a lead source. It rarely produces leads directly |
| Give third-party proof enterprise buyers ask for | Buy editorial coverage. Editors decide, and paid "features" must be disclosed as sponsored (FTC) |
| Feed sources AI assistants read | Fix weak positioning, a leaky funnel, or a product that does not retain customers |
| Help hiring and fundraising | Guarantee placements. A guarantee is a red flag |
| Win a narrative in a new category by educating reporters early | Show a precise ROI multiple. Triangulate instead |

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: press happens when news happens; no media list, narrative, or spokesperson prep.
- Tier 2 Building: a 12-month news calendar, a tiered list of 30 to 80 reporters, personalized pitches, a coverage log, an owner.
- Tier 3 Operating: objectives set before activity, share of voice against three to five competitors, message pull-through, an annual data asset, 48 hour amplification.
- Tier 4 Compounding: measurement reaches pipeline impact, AI citations tracked, annual perception research.

**First build, in order**
1. Write a one-page narrative (3 to 5 hours): what is changing in the buyer's world, who is hurt by the old way, what the new way looks like, why now, why you, and three proof points (a customer result with a number, a product fact, a data point).
2. Build a 20-outlet list from your customers (3 to 4 hours). Ask five to ten what they read, subscribe to, or listen to for work. Add where competitors appear. Per outlet, find the one writer or host who covered your topic in the last 90 days and note a recent story. Trade newsletters and niche podcasts often beat national business press.
3. Publish a minimum newsroom page (2 hours): a 50 to 100 word boilerplate, founder bio and headshot, logos, three product images, key facts, a monitored press email.
4. Be useful before asking (1 to 2 hours a week for six weeks): answer reporter requests in your field within hours, comment on target reporters' work, offer one a data point for a story they are writing.
5. Pitch one angle to five reporters (4 to 6 hours). Individual notes under 150 words, one follow-up after 3 to 5 business days. Pitch a trend or insight, not an announcement. Test: would the reporter still care with your company name removed?
6. When you have three relevant placements, two reporters who replied, and a plan for two quarters, build the standard program: a message house (umbrella message, three pillars, proof points, a "what we will not say" list), a news calendar (one earned moment a quarter at seed, one a month at Series B, filled with original data and trend comment), spokesperson training with a pre-approved positions document on 10 to 15 topics, templates for pitch, release, and run-of-show, and a 48 hour amplification checklist.
7. Write an issues list (security incident, outage, layoffs, lawsuit, viral complaint, executive exit) and hand anything that fits the crisis definition to FW-COMMS-001. Nobody comments to media on those without the crisis owner.

**Decision rules**
- If awareness is the constraint and positioning is clear, do PR: narrative, 20-outlet list, five pitches within 30 days.
- If positioning is the constraint, defer pitching. Keep only a newsroom page and approved facts for inbound reporters.
- If conversion is the constraint, build only the amplification workflow for coverage you have and put the top five placements into proposals and late-stage emails.
- If someone offers a "guaranteed placement" or paid "feature," decline.
- If you are founder-led with no marketer, run only the first five steps, learn the terms before any interview (on the record, on background, off the record, exclusive, embargo), and sign no retainer or wire package beyond a funding announcement.
- If you consider an agency, confirm first: a written narrative, a 12-month calendar with at least four earned moments, a spokesperson with 4+ hours a month, and two referenceable customers. Otherwise you pay for a retainer with nothing to sell. Start with a project. Selection lives in FW-OPS-001. If one is already on retainer without these, do not renew or expand. Rescope to named Tier A outlets with a 90 day checkpoint.
- If you choose between exclusive and embargo: exclusive when one outlet reaches most of your ICP and the story is moderate; embargo to three to ten outlets when the story is big. Never offer one exclusive twice. Confirm terms in writing with date, time, and time zone.
- If you use a wire, use it for a formal public record (funding, acquisitions, regulated news), not minor updates, and do not count on wire links for SEO.
- If you sell enterprise, plan 2 to 6 months for named customer approvals. Choose analyst relations over PR when buyers need analyst validation to shortlist (FW-INFLUENCE-001). Choose PR when they rely on peer and media signals.
- If you are regulated, build a counsel-reviewed positions document before pitching, add 1 to 3 weeks of compliance review, and get written approval for every named customer. If an IPO is under consideration, securities counsel governs what you can say.
- If you hire, finish the narrative, calendar, and spokesperson commitment first. Hire full-time when earned moments exceed one a month. A freelance publicist fits 4 to 12 moments a year. Move in-house when agency fees pass about $15,000 a month for 12 months or more.
- If the founder is the bottleneck, train two or three more spokespeople and set a response time: acknowledge in one business hour, answer within four hours on deadline.
- If a release needs a quote, write one that states an opinion, a sourced number, or a consequence. If it could appear in any company's release, rewrite it.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Media list | 20 outlets to start. Tier A 10 to 15 contacts, Tier B 20 to 40 | Lintel guidance |
| Tier A placements per quarter | Seed 1 to 4. Series A 3 to 8. Series B 8 to 20 | Lintel guidance |
| Pitch reply rate | Targeted pitches commonly 10 to 25%. Mass pitching often under 2% | Lintel guidance, varies |
| Message pull-through | 40 to 70% of relevant coverage carries a key message | Lintel guidance |
| Self-reported media attribution | 3 to 15% of inbound for active programs. Needs a free-text "how did you hear about us" field | Lintel guidance |
| Approvals | 24 hours for reactive quotes, 5 business days for releases | Lintel guidance |
| Monthly budget | Seed $0 to $3,000. Series A $3,000 to $15,000. Series B $10,000 to $35,000. Freelance publicist $2,000 to $8,000. Boutique agency $8,000 to $20,000 | Lintel guidance, varies widely |
| ROI horizons | 6 months: branded search and self-reported attribution. 12 months: influenced pipeline and win-rate gap. 24 months: share of voice | Lintel guidance. Do not promise a multiple |

**Measurement standard.** Set objectives before activity and report outputs, outcomes, and impact, not clip counts. This follows the Barcelona Principles and the Integrated Evaluation Framework (AMEC). Drop advertising value equivalency and "potential reach," which the Barcelona Principles reject. Before the first announcement, set up referral reporting in analytics and a brand-search filter in Search Console, because those periods cannot be measured later.

**Where it goes wrong:**
- Announcements with no story. The pitch frames an internal milestone, not what it tells readers about the market.
- A 300 to 1,000 contact blast from a database: replies under 1%, burned relationships.
- Embargo or exclusive breaks from missing written terms or an early leak by a customer, investor, or job post.
- Coverage sits unused: sales never saw it and it is not in proposals or sequences.
- An unprepared spokesperson gives long, promotional answers or says something unapproved.
