# Newer and situational plays

Fourteen plays that only some B2B software companies should start now. For a marketer or founder at seed to Series B deciding whether a play is right yet, what to build first, and when to stop.

**Owns:** Is this play right for us now, the first build, the numbers that say it is working, and the kill conditions.
**Does not own:** Pricing model and price level (FW-GROWTH-005) · discount approval and deal terms (FW-SALES-006) · the full category creation program (FW-PM-007) · running each analyst interaction (FW-INFLUENCE-001) · launch execution (FW-PM-003) · GTM motion design (FW-FOUND-005) · competitive research and battlecards (FW-PM-004) · win/loss interview method (FW-PM-006) · retaining current customers (FW-CUSTMKT-004) · a podcast you own (FW-PODCAST-001 to 003) · general conversion testing method (FW-DEMANDGEN-004, FW-GROWTH-002).

## AI in the marketing workflow  (FW-EMERGING-001)

**When this matters:** More than 25% of team time goes to repeatable low-judgment work (first drafts, data pulls, report assembly, briefs) and headcount is flat. Or you are sizing a first marketing hire.

**Skip it when:** There is no documented ICP, defined messaging, or repeatable GTM motion. AI speeds execution, it cannot supply a strategy. Also skip with no performance baseline to optimize against, or in a regulated vertical with no legal clearance.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: individuals use one chat tool for drafts. No shared prompts, no governance.
- Tier 2 Building: shared stack and prompt library, AI-first workflows for the top five content types.
- Tier 3 Operating: a written operating model (approved tools, review rules per content type, escalation path) plus AI in reporting.
- Tier 4 Compounding: AI in the measurement layer (predictive scoring, budget optimization, attribution) with agentic workflows reviewed at checkpoints, not per task.

**First build, in order**
1. Pick the three highest-volume repeatable weekly tasks. Pilot one tool on each for 30 days, recording hours before and after.
2. Audit the full workflow. Tag each step high AI substitution, AI-assisted with human judgment, or human-only.
3. Name one AI stack owner before buying anything new. The bottleneck at small team size is the operating model, not tool access.
4. Build the shared prompt library, automate recurring reports, then write the governance doc.

**Decision rules**
- If seed and founder-led, build only the content stack: one language model, 10 prompts, one quality gate. Under $150 a month, 2.0x to 2.5x output within 60 days.
- If seed with one marketer, build the full content stack plus automated reporting and skip lead-scoring AI. There is not enough conversion data to train it.
- If Series B and the gap is pipeline volume, put AI into lead scoring and analytics before more content.
- If regulated and governance is not cleared, use AI internally only, then get pre-approved content templates so output inside a template needs a spot check instead of full review.
- If the team uses AI but the output multiple is under 1.2x, diagnose the workflow rather than buying another tool.
- Never put AI in front of trust-sensitive external communication, executive voice, crisis messaging, or compliance-sensitive claims.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Output multiple vs. pre-AI baseline | Tier 2 1.5x to 2.0x · Tier 3 2.0x to 3.5x · Tier 4 3.5x+ | Under 1.2x means no real gain |
| Workflow AI coverage | Tier 1 under 20% · Tier 2 30% to 50% · Tier 3 60% to 80% · Tier 4 80%+ | Lintel guidance |
| AI share of marketing budget | 3% to 8% at Tier 2 to 3 | Under 2% underinvestment, over 12% tool sprawl |
| Time per asset | Blog post 2 to 4 hrs (6 to 12 without) · 5 ad variants 30 to 60 min (3 to 5 hrs) | Lintel guidance |
| Lead-score agreement with sales | Rule-based 55% to 65% · AI predictive after training 72% to 82% | Needs 6+ months of clean data |
| Prompt library | 25+ prompts at 70% use by Tier 2 · 50+ at 80% by Tier 3 | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** Buying tools without redesigning the workflow. Publishing output with no quality gate. Running AI on an unvalidated strategy. Governance written for control instead of scale, so people route around it.

## Product-led growth marketing  (FW-EMERGING-002)

**When this matters:** The product delivers real value within minutes or hours with no mandatory sales involvement, activation is under 40% and nobody owns raising it, or sales is working deals under $5K ACV that could close without them.

**Skip it when:** The product needs configuration or services before value. The buyer does not use the product. ACV is above $50K with legal, security, and executive sign-off every time. Fit is unconfirmed, because optimizing a PLG funnel before fit accelerates churn.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: a free tier or trial with no defined activation moment and no instrumentation.
- Tier 2 Building: activation defined and instrumented, with a conversion sequence triggered by activation status.
- Tier 3 Operating: a product qualified lead (PQL) model routes high-intent users to sales in the CRM.
- Tier 4 Compounding: viral and sharing loops instrumented, with acquisition, activation, and expansion tested together.

**First build, in order**
1. Define the activation moment: the in-product action that predicts conversion and retention. Instrument it before any marketing program.
2. Measure activation rate, time to activate, and time to first value.
3. Build onboarding keyed to activation status and conversion-predicting behavior, not a feature tour.
4. Score acquisition sources by activation rate, not signups, and cut sources below 15%.
5. Build the PQL model and routing rule, sized so 5 reps get 50 to 100 PQLs a month.

**Decision rules**
- If activation is below 20%, fix activation before spending another dollar upstream. Everything else leaks out.
- If free-to-paid is under 3%, check the trigger, then the upgrade moment, then price.
- If net revenue retention is under 90%, treat PLG as a retention problem, not acquisition.
- If PQLs close at the same rate as cold outbound, the criteria are wrong. A PQL starts with an active user and should close at 2x to 3x.
- If ACV is above $20K and PLG found champions inside large accounts, hand those accounts to ABM as Tier 1 targets.
- The PLG pricing page belongs to FW-EMERGING-013: publish real prices including the entry tier, and state the free-to-paid boundary as a limit, not an adjective.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Activation rate | Under 20% problem · 20% to 35% early average · 35% to 50% good · above 50% for ICP-fit users strong | Lintel guidance |
| Time to activate / first value | Under 1 day best-in-class, over 7 days a friction problem · first value under 30 min excellent, over 72 hrs problematic | Lintel guidance |
| Free-to-paid conversion | Under 3% broken · 3% to 6% average · 6% to 10% good · above 10% strong | Lintel guidance |
| Net revenue retention | Under 90% contraction · 100% to 110% good · 110% to 120% strong | Lintel guidance |
| Viral coefficient | Under 0.1 negligible · 0.3 to 0.5 a real channel · above 1.0 self-sustaining | Lintel guidance |
| PQLs | 50 to 100 a month per 5 reps · sales acceptance above 65% | Lintel guidance |
| Expansion share of new revenue | Under 15% weak · 30% to 50% strong | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** No defined activation moment, so nothing downstream is measurable. Onboarding that teaches features instead of driving activation. Upgrade prompts triggered by day count rather than behavior. Every signup routed to sales. PLG numbers reported as marketing metrics instead of revenue metrics.

*Credit: product-led growth entered broad use after OpenView's 2019 research on product-led companies.*

## Account-based marketing  (FW-EMERGING-003)

**When this matters:** ACV above $20K and the top 20 customers are more than 40% of ARR. Pipeline coverage under 3x with no explanation. Fewer than 5,000 realistic target accounts. Cycles of 90 days or more with multiple stakeholders.

**Skip it when:** ACV is below $5K. The ICP is not validated (under 10 closed-won deals). Sales has no capacity to act on signals, because unworked signals waste the whole spend. Marketing headcount is below two.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: a list exists, sales does outbound to it, marketing runs nothing coordinated.
- Tier 2 Building: 50 to 100 accounts with intent monitoring, alerts, a weekly sales review, and an agreed definition of "engaged".
- Tier 3 Operating: three tiers running (1:1 personalized, 1:few clustered, 1:many programmatic) with budget per tier.
- Tier 4 Compounding: connected to the full revenue cycle, including PQL upsell from PLG and expansion ABM.

**First build, in order**
1. Co-build a 50 to 100 account list with sales from ICP criteria. Built alone it gets ignored.
2. Agree in writing what "engaged" means and the SLA for sales follow-up on a signal.
3. Add first-party intent monitoring and alerts, then run one channel only: company-level targeting plus one personalized outreach per Tier 1 account.
4. Write account briefs for the 10 to 15 Tier 1 accounts (15 to 20 minutes each AI-assisted).
5. Add pipeline-type and competitor fields so ABM pipeline reports separately. Nothing is fundable without this.

**Decision rules**
- If ACV is below $5K, do not run ABM at all. Route to demand generation. Between $5K and $20K, run only list-level advertising to the account list, with no per-account personalization: a $2,000 per-account program against a $15K deal is a 13% cost of acquisition before any sales time. The one exception is an account whose logo would be a marquee reference.
- If Series A with ACV above $20K at Tier 1, run 10 to 15 Tier 1 accounts only, single channel, no ABM platform. Target 2 to 3 closed target-account deals before scaling.
- If one marketer and ACV above $30K, cap at 50 accounts with 10 in Tier 1 and a 30-minute weekly sales review. A list above 300 accounts at Tier 1 or 2 is over-scoped.
- If target-account win rate is not above overall win rate at Tier 2, pull the last 10 losses. Price or product gaps sit outside ABM. Competitor preference means the personalization is not differentiating.
- If the gap is immediate pipeline, pair ABM with short-cycle demand generation. ABM builds over 3 to 6 months.
- Minimum viable stack with minimal budget and ACV above $30K: under $3,000 a month.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Target-account share of pipeline | Tier 2 25% to 40% · Tier 3 35% to 50% · under 15% means it is not working | Lintel guidance |
| Win rate on ABM accounts | 1.3x to 2x overall · above 40% on a well-run 20-account Tier 1 | Lintel guidance |
| Sales cycle | 20% to 35% shorter than non-ABM deals of similar size | Equal or slower means no real engagement |
| Account engagement | 30% to 50% show a signal per quarter at Tier 2 · under 20% is targeting or channel | Lintel guidance |
| Intent signal to opportunity (90 days) | 20% to 35% · under 15% means thresholds are too sensitive | Lintel guidance |
| Deal size premium | 15% to 30% over non-ABM | No premium means account selection is wrong |
| Cost per ABM opportunity | $3,000 to $8,000 at Series B with $50K ACV · up to $15,000 at Series C with $100K+ | Lintel guidance |
| ROI by tier | Tier 1 above 5:1 · Tier 2 above 3:1 · Tier 3 above 2:1 | Lintel guidance |
| Budget | Series A $3K to $8K/mo · Series B $15K to $30K/mo · Series C+ $40K to $100K+/mo | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** A logo wish list instead of an ICP match. Sales not acting on signals. Personalization that uses the company name but no company context. Too many accounts for the money. Measuring on marketing metrics. Pointing ABM at accounts already in pipeline.

## Partners, ecosystems, marketplaces, and affiliates  (FW-EMERGING-004, FW-EMERGING-010)

FW-EMERGING-004 owns partner programs, co-marketing, marketplace and app store listings, and co-sell. FW-EMERGING-010 owns affiliates. If deals are enterprise, multi-stakeholder, and salesperson-closed, cookie attribution misfires: use a partner program with deal registration, not affiliates.

**When this matters:** Deals were already referred by third parties with no system behind it. You are absent from marketplaces your buyers use and competitors are present. Direct CAC is rising. For affiliates: publishers rank for "best [category]" and "alternatives to [competitor]" with competitor links, and your own traffic converts at roughly 1% or better.

**Skip it when:** Fit is unproven. Sales cannot support partner pipeline. There is no portal, partner pricing, or enablement yet. The product changes significantly in the next 6 months. For affiliates: the maximum commission your margin supports is under roughly $20 per acquisition, there is no conversion tracking, or nobody has 20 hours a month.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: a list of signed partners and a discount. Partner pipeline under 5%.
- Tier 2 Building: a defined program (types, tiers, margin model, minimum enablement) piloted with 5 to 10 partners.
- Tier 3 Operating: distinct tracks (reseller, technology, services), a portal, market development funds (MDF), and a partner marketing calendar.
- Tier 4 Compounding: marketplace presence, co-sell with anchor platform vendors, and partner revenue as a named share of total.

**First build, in order**
1. Audit closed-won data for informal partner influence, then pick the 2 to 3 partner types already producing pipeline.
2. Set economics before recruiting: referral commission commonly 10% to 15%, active referral 20% to 25%, reseller margin 20% to 35%. For affiliates, write down the maximum commission per acquisition first.
3. Build the activation sequence (onboarding series, asset package, a worked earnings example, a named contact, a 30-day goal) and run dormant partners through it before recruiting anyone new.
4. Pilot with 5 to 10 partners and measure activation, not signatures.
5. Stand the marketplace listing up properly (optimized copy, current screenshots, reviews, a certified integration, a co-sell agreement) and maintain it on a schedule.
6. Add deal registration and a partner-sourced pipeline field before asking for budget. Run an affiliate incrementality holdout before scaling spend.

**Decision rules**
- Partner type follows ACV: above $100K services and systems integrators, $20K to $100K technology integrations, below $20K referral only. Reseller margin does not work below roughly $5K ACV.
- If partner CAC approaches direct CAC, the commission or MDF rate is too high for the pipeline produced.
- If under 25% of enrolled partners are active in 90 days, the problem is enablement. Do not recruit more.
- If the top 3 partners are more than 60% of partner pipeline, that is concentration risk. Target no single partner above 20%.
- If affiliate incrementality tests under 40%, the mix is wrong or the attribution window is too long. Discount and brand-term partners frequently test at 10% to 20%, which is interception, not growth.
- If GTM is partner-led, freeze packaging changes to at most one a year. Every change breaks partner enablement.
- A listing producing under 5 qualified leads a month is a listing problem, not a channel problem.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Partner-sourced pipeline | Tier 2 10% to 20% · Tier 3 20% to 35% · Tier 4 35% to 50% | Under 5% with a live program means it is not functioning |
| Partner-sourced win rate and velocity | 1.2x to 2x overall win rate · deals close 15% to 30% faster | Below overall win rate means low-quality referrals |
| Partner CAC | 30% to 60% of direct CAC | Lintel guidance |
| Active partner rate (90 days) | 40% to 60% healthy · under 25% an enablement gap | Lintel guidance |
| MDF | Utilization 65% to 80% · $3 to $6 pipeline per $1 · total MDF 1% to 3% of program revenue | Below $2:1 means MDF funds awareness |
| Marketplace listing output | 15 to 40 qualified leads a month with real category search volume | Lintel guidance |
| Partner NPS | Above 40 good, above 60 excellent, below 20 an attrition risk | NPS from Reichheld |
| Affiliate activation and conversion | 40%+ activation with a real sequence (10% to 20% without) · content publishers convert 1% to 5%, newsletters 0.5% to 3% | Lintel guidance |
| Affiliate commission-to-margin | Must sit below gross margin with room for fees. 20% on a 75% margin product works, 45% on 55% does not | Lintel guidance |
| Program budget | Tier 2 $20K to $50K/yr · Tier 3 $150K to $400K/yr · Tier 4 $500K to $2M+/yr | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** Recruiting before enabling. A program with no economics a partner can model. A portal nobody visits. Co-marketing designed as awareness instead of lead generation. A listing with old screenshots and no reviews. For affiliates: non-incremental partners cannibalizing demand you already created, fraud, and missing disclosure compliance.

## Category path and analyst coverage  (FW-EMERGING-005)

Owns the category path decision and what the analyst program is for. Once creation is chosen, FW-PM-007 owns naming and the program. FW-INFLUENCE-001 owns how to run each briefing and inquiry.

**When this matters:** Sales loses deals to "we went with the incumbent" or "we're not sure your category fits us". Analysts describe you in a competitor's vocabulary and prospects hear that first. You are entering a market with no dominant label, or moving upmarket where inclusion in a ranked vendor report is a stated enterprise requirement.

**Skip it when:** Pre-fit. Below $3M ARR, sales-led, with no enterprise deals in flight, because analyst reports do not move buyers at that volume. Buyers are developers or bottoms-up PLG users. There is no ICP or messaging architecture to anchor to.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: no written category hypothesis. Others define the box you sit in.
- Tier 2 Building: 2 to 4 nearest analysts identified, with one unsolicited education-only briefing sent.
- Tier 3 Operating: the path is decided and a category narrative exists naming the problem, why existing categories fail, and the vocabulary analysts can reuse.
- Tier 4 Compounding: a named owner, a quarterly briefing calendar, an inquiry strategy, participation in ranked vendor reports, and a mindshare dashboard.

**First build, in order**
1. Write a one-paragraph category hypothesis: the problem, the category it falls into today, and whether you want to own or reframe it.
2. Pick the path in writing: category entry, category redesign (a named subcategory), or category creation.
3. Send one unsolicited briefing request to the 2 to 4 nearest analysts, education only, with no report ask.
4. Write the category narrative, then translate it into sales enablement within 90 days. A narrative sales cannot say is not a narrative.
5. Fund original research before any paid analyst relationship. A credible buyer survey costs $10K to $25K and is the best-return spend at Series A to B.

**Decision rules**
- Category creation needs at least $500K a year of dedicated investment and a three-year commitment. If that cannot be confirmed, shift to category redesign.
- If seed and founder-led, the only deliverable is the hypothesis. No narrative document, no program, no contracts.
- If Series A, PLG, and SMB, deprioritize analyst relations and use category strategy for SEO and content alignment.
- If Series B, sales-led, and enterprise, analyst relations is required: 8 to 12 briefings a quarter, original research, an inquiry strategy, one named owner.
- Never buy a service contract before the narrative exists or without a written inquiry strategy. Unused inquiry hours are the top waste in these budgets.
- Name the category for buyers who will search it, not for analysts.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Analysts who describe you correctly | 40% of priority analysts at Tier 2 · 80%+ at Tier 3 | Lintel guidance |
| Your vocabulary in analyst research | 15% to 25% at Tier 2 · 40%+ at Tier 3 | Lintel guidance |
| Share of category coverage mentioning you | 10% to 15% at Tier 2 · 20% to 30% at Tier 3 | Lintel guidance |
| Coverage sentiment | 50%+ positive at Tier 3 · over 20% negative is a priority flag | Lintel guidance |
| Placement in ranked vendor reports | Mid-band at first inclusion is normal · top band within 2 to 3 research cycles if the program works | Lintel guidance |
| Win rate lift on analyst-influenced deals | 15 to 25 percentage points at Tier 3+ | Lintel guidance |
| Analyst-sourced inbound pipeline | 8% to 15% at Tier 3 · above 20% suggests over-reliance | Lintel guidance |
| Original research inbound | 200 to 500 net new contacts per publication at Series B · 500 to 1,500 at Series C+ | Lintel guidance |
| Budget | Series A $10K to $25K/yr (research only) · Series B $90K to $195K/yr · Series C+ $325K to $690K/yr. A major analyst firm service contract commonly runs $30K to $90K a year each | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** Framing the category around your product instead of the buyer's problem. Choosing creation without category capital. Treating analyst relations as a deal tool rather than market development. Inconsistent vocabulary across site, sales, and press. Hedging by calling it several things for several audiences.

## Community-led growth  (FW-EMERGING-006)

**When this matters:** Word of mouth already drives inbound and nothing captures it. Your product serves a practitioner identity people identify with socially (marketers, engineers, ops, designers, security). Competitor communities already produce peer recommendations your reps meet in deals.

**Skip it when:** Pre-fit. The buyer is non-social (CFOs, general counsel, niche industrial). The motion is fully enterprise top-down. You have fewer than 200 active users, because below that it is an empty room that repels. Nobody can own it full time.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: no owned space and no idea where advocates already gather.
- Tier 2 Building: a minimum viable space with 20 to 30 founding advocates, a written purpose, and one weekly ritual.
- Tier 3 Operating: a tracked path from participation to trial or demo, plus a public series non-members can sample.
- Tier 4 Compounding: community-influenced pipeline, referrals, and retention measured by cohort.

**First build, in order**
1. Ask your 20 to 30 most engaged customers where they already gather. Go there first. Build nothing.
2. Launch the space with those advocates, a one-sentence purpose, and one reliable weekly touchpoint. Build participation, not features.
3. Build new-member onboarding before promoting the community. It roughly doubles 30-day activation.
4. Add the bridge to trial or demo, with at least two bridge touchpoints per 30 days of programming.
5. Integrate member records with the CRM, then report influenced pipeline, referrals, and retention by cohort. Member count is a denominator, not a result.

**Decision rules**
- If monthly active member rate is below 15%, you have a notification list. Fix programming and activation before recruiting.
- If staff are first responder on more than half of questions, members never get the chance. Slow down deliberately.
- If first response time exceeds 48 hours, repeat posting collapses.
- If community-sourced pipeline is under 10%, you are running a success channel. Promote the community before the sale, not after.
- If champions are picked on posting volume instead of how many members they connect, the identity loop will not run.
- Do not add a second channel until the first is healthy.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly active member rate | 25% to 35% under 5,000 members · 20% to 30% above 10,000 · below 15% is a notification list | Lintel guidance |
| Contributor rate | 5% to 12% of members · ambassadors 1% to 3% | Lintel guidance |
| Member-to-member answer rate | 60% to 80% at Tier 3 · above 80% at Tier 4 · 40% to 60% is the regulated ceiling | Lintel guidance |
| First response time | Under 24 hrs at Tier 2 to 3 · under 4 hrs at Tier 4 · over 48 hrs suppresses posting | Lintel guidance |
| 30-day new member activation | 30% to 50% with an onboarding sequence · under 20% without | Lintel guidance |
| Member referral rate | 10% to 25% at Tier 3 · under 5% means the identity loop is not running | Lintel guidance |
| Community-sourced pipeline | 25% to 45% at Tier 3 · under 10% means it is a success channel | Lintel guidance |
| Community-sourced close rate | 1.5x to 3x the site average for the same stage | Members arrive informed |
| Budget | Series A 3% to 6% of marketing · Series B 6% to 10% · cost per active member $4 to $25/mo | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** Launching an empty room. Staff dominating every thread, so it reads as vendor-captured. Engagement with no revenue connection. Scaling member count before the activation foundation exists. Depending on the founder to carry every conversation.

## Borrowed audiences: creators, YouTube, and audio  (FW-EMERGING-007, FW-EMERGING-009, FW-EMERGING-017)

FW-EMERGING-007 owns creator relationships, FW-EMERGING-009 owns your own YouTube channel as a searchable asset, and FW-EMERGING-017 owns paid and earned audio you do not own.

**When this matters:** Your buyers follow specific creators, channels, or shows and none mention you. Paid social is flattening on cold traffic. Your product demonstrates better than it reads and prospects search YouTube for tutorials and comparisons in your category. You can name at least 5 podcasts your best customers listen to.

**Skip it when:** Buyers are C-suite in regulated industries buying through formal procurement, or ACV is above $250K with a fully institutional committee. ICP and messaging are undefined, because borrowed audiences amplify the message you have. There is no capture path downstream. For video: fewer than 2 people can own production consistently. For audio: you cannot fund roughly $5,000 to $25,000 across 3 to 5 shows, or the product cannot be explained in 60 seconds of speech.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: no named creators, no keyword list, no attribution field. Occasional one-off placements.
- Tier 2 Building: 3 creators approached value-first, 5 keyword-targeted videos published, one structured audio test across 3 to 5 shows.
- Tier 3 Operating: a tracked creator pilot, at least 2 videos a month across three content types, an audio show portfolio with renewal rules.
- Tier 4 Compounding: a tiered creator program with a named owner, YouTube run with view-to-pipeline attribution, always-on audio on blended attribution.

**First build, in order**
1. Add a free-text "How did you hear about us?" field and survey 20 recent customers on which creators and shows they follow. Without self-reported attribution none of these channels can be read.
2. List 10 creators whose audiences overlap your ICP and spend two weeks consuming their content as a researcher.
3. Find the 10 highest-traffic YouTube searches in your category before producing a single video, then publish 5 videos on the top tutorial and comparison queries with keyword-rich titles, chapters, and one clear next step each.
4. Approach 3 creators with a value-first offer before any sponsorship ask, then pilot with 2 to 3 across micro and mid-tier with tracked URLs or codes.
5. Run the audio test properly: 3 to 5 shows, 3 to 4 host-read mid-roll episodes each, with a vanity URL, a promo code, and self-reported attribution, against a CPA target set in advance.

**Decision rules**
- Never judge audio on a single episode. Most listeners search your brand name rather than typing a code, so multiply tracked response by the observed promo-code multiplier (commonly 2 to 5).
- Lead creator relationships with value, not a rate card. A $500 micro-creator relationship with genuine fit beats a $15,000 macro sponsorship without it. Relationships under 3 months transfer no credibility.
- If sponsored engagement runs more than 30% below that creator's organic engagement, the audience has detected and discounted it.
- If video click-through in search impressions is below 3%, fix thumbnail and title before content. If retention is below 25% on a tutorial, fix the hook or the title promise.
- Keep affiliate creators earning at least $500 a month. Below $200 they reduce promotion frequency. No single creator above 35% of creator-attributed pipeline.
- Publish video on a schedule. Six videos in three weeks then nothing signals neglect and the channel stalls.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Creator CAC | 30% to 60% below blended CAC at Tier 3 · below paid social CAC at Tier 2 | Lintel guidance |
| Creator-attributed pipeline | 8% to 12% at Tier 2 · 12% to 20% at Tier 3 · 20%+ at Tier 4 | Lintel guidance |
| Click-through by format | YouTube sponsored segment 1% to 3% · newsletter 2% to 5% · LinkedIn sponsored 0.5% to 2% · podcast dedicated read 3% to 8% | Lintel guidance |
| Video retention and CTR | Above 40% retention on educational tutorials · 4% to 7% CTR in search, 2% to 4% in browse | Lintel guidance |
| YouTube-to-site sessions | 200 to 500/mo at Tier 2 · 1,000 to 3,000 at Tier 3 · 5,000 to 15,000 at Tier 4 | Lintel guidance |
| Video CTA click rate | 0.5% to 2% top-of-funnel · 2% to 5% comparison content · above 5% direct demo CTAs | Lintel guidance |
| Video CAC | Below blended CAC by Tier 3 · best programs 50% to 70% below | Production is one-time, views compound |
| Podcast CPM | Host-read $18 to $50 · niche B2B $50 to $100+ · programmatic audio $5 to $20 · flat-fee niche episodes $500 to $5,000 | Verify current market |
| Audio response | Roughly 1 to 5 site visits per 1,000 downloads for matched host-read · branded search lift 5% to 20% · self-reported attribution grows to 5% to 20% of new customers after 6+ months | Lintel guidance |
| Budget | Creator Year 1 cash $2,000 to $15,000 · Video Year 1 $2,760 to $6,960 (equipment $70 to $230, editing $80 to $150/video), Tier 3 $57K to $104K/yr · Audio 5% to 15% of marketing once validated | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** Picking creators on reach instead of audience fit. Leading with a rate card. Over-scripting so the audience discounts it. One-and-done sponsorships. No attribution infrastructure, so the channel cannot be defended. Publishing video without keyword research. Buying downloads instead of buyers. Renewing a show out of habit.

## Revenue intelligence and the technical sale  (FW-EMERGING-008, FW-EMERGING-014)

FW-EMERGING-008 owns the signal layer: lead definitions, scoring, routing, intent, and forecast quality. FW-EMERGING-014 owns the technical proof layer: the sales engineer (SE) model, demos, proofs of concept, and technical content. Enablement content from a product marketing view is FW-PM-005. Sales stages are FW-SALES-003.

**When this matters:** Reps prospect accounts that never convert while warm inbound goes stale. Forecast accuracy is off by more than 20%. Sales ignores leads because the MQL-to-SQL definition is disputed. For the technical layer: deals stall at "validate technical fit", win rates on technically evaluated deals run 15 to 30 points lower, proofs arrive with no scope or decision date, and security questionnaires are answered from scratch every time.

**Skip it when:** Fewer than 3 reps, so the founder can prioritize by hand. CRM accuracy on company, title, and stage is below 60%, because intelligence on dirty data amplifies noise. Fully self-serve PLG. For the technical layer: a rep can demo end to end in 20 minutes unprepared, ACV is below roughly $5,000 to $10,000 on a high-volume motion, or there are fewer than three or four sellers.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: no written MQL or SQL definition. Technical questions answered ad hoc, often by the founder.
- Tier 2 Building: the top 3 intent signals instrumented and routed within 24 hours, plus SE rules of engagement and a required discovery-to-demo map.
- Tier 3 Operating: a fit-plus-behavior lead score validated against closed-won data, SLA-driven routing, and a mandatory written proof agreement with success criteria and a go/no-go date.
- Tier 4 Compounding: conversation intelligence, third-party intent, predictive pipeline scoring, a self-service technical evaluation layer, and a monthly SE-to-marketing loop.

**First build, in order**
1. Write the MQL and SQL definitions with sales. This resolves more friction than any tool.
2. List every technical question from the last ten deals, group duplicates, and write one approved page of answers to the top five plus a one-page architecture overview.
3. Instrument the top 3 behavioral intent signals (pricing visits, trial milestones, high-frequency engagement) and route them to the right rep within 24 hours.
4. Write SE rules of engagement (minimum deal size, required stage, required discovery) and require a discovery-to-demo map before every demo.
5. Make a written proof agreement with success criteria and a decision date mandatory, then publish the self-service technical layer (integration docs, architecture guide, trust page, interactive tour).

**Decision rules**
- Respond to demo requests within 5 minutes during business hours. Median under 10 minutes for demo requests, under 2 hours for MQLs.
- If a lead score has never been validated against closed-won data, do not route on it.
- If forecast accuracy is worse than plus or minus 20%, fix the signals before the forecast process. If reps ignore alerts, raise the threshold rather than adding channels.
- If more than 20% of proofs end with no decision, the criteria were not testable or a stakeholder arrived late. Add a stakeholder map to proof entry.
- If SE utilization is above 90%, the engagement gate is too loose. Below 60%, redirect capacity to demo engineering and content.
- Buy a security questionnaire tool once the library passes roughly 150 questions. Never outsource security answers or architecture claims. Parallelize security review with technical validation.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| MQL to SQL | 20% to 30% at Tier 2 · 30% to 40% at Tier 3 · below 15% means the threshold is too low | Lintel guidance |
| SLA compliance and response | Above 80% on MQLs, above 90% on demo requests · median under 10 min on demo requests | 5 minutes is the target |
| Pipeline coverage and forecast accuracy | 3x to 4x mature, 4x to 5x earlier-stage · ±20% at Tier 2, ±15% Tier 3, ±10% Tier 4 | Lintel guidance |
| Opportunity-to-close | 40% to 60% qualified inbound · 20% to 35% outbound or event-sourced | Inbound typically beats outbound by 10 to 20 points |
| Intent-sourced opportunity rate | 8% to 15% for multi-signal programs · below 5% means poor data or a permissive threshold | Lintel guidance |
| Technical-evaluation win rate | 35% to 55% mid-market · 25% to 45% enterprise with bake-offs | Lintel guidance |
| Demo-to-next-step | 50% to 65% on qualified deals · below 40% is a demo or discovery problem | Lintel guidance |
| Proof win rate and duration | 60% to 75% win rate · 14 to 21 days mid-market, 21 to 45 enterprise · over 50% past plan is scope creep | Lintel guidance |
| Security questionnaire turnaround | 1 to 3 days with a library · 7 to 15 days without | Lintel guidance |
| SE utilization and load | 65% to 85% healthy · 8 to 20 hrs per closed-won mid-market deal, 25 to 60 enterprise | Lintel guidance |

| Technical content budget | Series A $5K to $25K/yr · Series B $25K to $100K/yr · Series C+ $100K to $400K/yr | Published asset $500 to $3,000 outsourced |

**Where it goes wrong:** Treating a signal routing problem as a content problem and producing more battlecards. A lead score never validated. Alert fatigue that trains reps to ignore the system. Intent data used as a spam trigger. SEs arriving late with no discovery and giving a product tour. Proofs with no written success criteria. Technical content locked behind a form, away from the self-directed buyer who wanted it.

## Pricing page and packaging  (FW-EMERGING-013)

Owns packaging and the page: how many tiers, what sits in each, published tier prices, page conversion, and moving customers to a new structure. "What should we charge and how do we make money" goes to FW-GROWTH-005, including price increases on existing customers and choosing a value metric for the first time. "Who can approve this discount" goes to FW-SALES-006. If discounts are high and similar across reps inside one tier, it is a packaging problem and belongs here. If they vary by rep, it belongs to the deal desk.

**When this matters:** The page gets 500 or more visits a month but under 2% to 3% convert. Sales routinely discounts deals that sit squarely inside a published tier. Prospects ask "which plan is right for us" on first calls. More than 10% to 15% of new customers change tier within 90 days. The product, segment, or pricing model changed in the last two quarters and the page did not.

**Skip it when:** Pre-fit, because any structure built now gets rebuilt within two quarters. Every deal closes through custom enterprise negotiation. Traffic is under roughly 500 visits a month, so demand is the constraint. No willingness-to-pay evidence exists and no interview access is available in 30 days. The product surface is mid-replatform or mid-merger.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: prices set by founder judgment or copied from a competitor. Page conversion untracked. Discounts decided verbally and never aggregated.
- Tier 2 Building: tiers drawn from buyer segmentation rather than feature inventory, prices checked against willingness-to-pay evidence, conversion reviewed on a cadence, and an anchor chosen deliberately.
- Tier 3 Operating: at least two completed A/B tests with documented results, packaging updated with product releases, competitive pricing audited on a schedule, and conversion joined to close rate by tier in the CRM.
- Tier 4 Compounding: willingness-to-pay research on a cycle, quantitative win/loss pricing analysis, tier-level retention and expansion reporting, and a revenue impact model before any packaging change ships.

**First build, in order**
1. Cluster your last 20 customers (company size, team size on product, main job hired for, what they pay, how they were sold). Find two to four clusters and write one sentence each that does not use the word "user". (2 to 3 hours.)
2. Run five pricing interviews, one per cluster, 25 minutes each. Ask what they expected to pay before seeing the price, what they compared you to, which option they almost chose, and what would have made the choice easier. (4 to 6 hours over two weeks.)
3. Diagnose before changing anything. Pull 90 days of page conversion, 90 days of discount by tier, 180-day tier change, and pricing themes from the last ten losses. Low conversion with healthy discounts and low tier churn is a page problem. Elevated discounts consistent across tiers is a price problem. High tier change plus "which plan" questions is a packaging problem. (2 days, and the most commonly skipped step.)
4. Draft two or three tiers plus an optional enterprise card. Per tier: a buyer-named name, one sentence on who it is for, the single capability that justifies the price, three to five supporting capabilities as outcomes with real limits, and the price. (3 hours.)
5. Validate against five closed-won and three closed-lost deals. If deals do not map cleanly, move the boundary. Do not add a tier.
6. Pick the anchor: the tier fitting the largest profitable segment, not the most expensive. Label it once, give it visual weight, write its value sentence first. Default to annual display with the monthly equivalent shown and the saving in both percent and currency.
7. Instrument before launch (page view, tier card interaction, table scroll, FAQ expansion, billing toggle, CTA click by tier, conversion complete), pass the selected tier to the CRM, and add free session recording. The pre-change baseline cannot be reconstructed later.
8. Run the 60-second clarity test with five target-matching people who have never seen the product. Four of five choosing the intended tier for the intended reason is a pass.
9. Brief sales and customer success before the page changes, agreeing grandfathering terms and the effective date in that session. Then publish and hold still for 30 days.

**Decision rules**
- If traffic is under 500 visits a month, publish a simple three-tier page with real prices and event tracking, then route effort to demand creation.
- If analytics are absent, stop and define the three events first. If the tier selected is not written to the CRM record, that field is the first build action, because close rate, discount, and retention by tier all depend on it and none can be backfilled.
- If fit is the constraint, hold price, keep two or three simple tiers, and use pricing conversations as discovery. The one exception is a broken value metric, where customers love the product and resent the bill.
- If asked "should we lower our price", require 90 days of average discount by tier and coded loss reasons separating "could not tell which plan fit" from "too expensive for the value". Recommend a packaging change before a price change whenever the first dominates.
- If asked whether to copy a competitor's pricing, say no. Copying imports their segmentation, cost base, and funding position. Use their page only for the questions buyers will arrive with.
- At Tier 2, run a Van Westendorp survey with 30 or more respondents on the tier with the highest discount rate (roughly 40% current customers, 30% recent buyers, 30% evaluated-and-did-not-buy), and set the working price at or slightly below the optimal point to absorb stated-preference bias.
- Only test at Tier 3 with traffic above 3,000 a month, one element at a time, sample size calculated first, nulls logged. If the sample is unreachable in four to eight weeks, run qualitative work instead.
- If discount rate in one tier rises three quarters in a row, treat the published price as unsupported. Reset list to the evidence and tighten approval thresholds at the same time.
- If a previous redesign "did not move conversion", do not redesign again. Check whether any buyer research preceded it. In nearly every recorded case the prior attempt changed presentation while the problem was structural.
- At pre-seed or seed, cap at three tiers, skip the comparison table, and expect to rebuild once. Spend the effort on the value metric, the most expensive decision to reverse.
- If a developer tool or API, publish unit prices, rate limits, and overage behavior precisely, give a calculator using the buyer's own inputs, keep a usable free tier, and remove "contact sales" below enterprise.
- If services-led, define tiers by deliverables, response times, and included hours, and publish what triggers a change order. Scope ambiguity is the churn mechanism.
- If regulated, publish compliance inclusions per tier and do not put mandatory controls only in the top tier when regulated mid-market buyers are a target. That turns a packaging choice into a disqualification.
- If hybrid PLG and sales, separate self-serve from sales-assisted visually and state the threshold (team size, volume, or compliance need) that tells a buyer which side they are on.
- Do not hire a dedicated pricing person at solo-marketer or founder-led scale. Assign packaging to product marketing or the founder with a quarterly review and buy research project by project.
- Keep comparison tables under roughly 25 to 30 rows, with specific values instead of undifferentiated check marks.
- **If leadership will not let you publish prices**, publish everything except the number: the tier structure, who each tier is for, what is included, the engagement model, the value metric, and a qualifying signal ("typically for teams of 200 or more"). Push for permission to show a starting-from figure or a typical range, because the alternative is that every buyer estimates for you and half estimate wrong. Measure page-to-request and request-to-qualified together: a page that raises requests while lowering qualification has failed, not succeeded.
- **Never ship an empty enterprise tile.** A card reading "Enterprise: contact sales" with no inclusions and no threshold makes enterprise visitors leave without a trace, so the loss never appears in analytics. The tell is a card under 20 words with enterprise form submissions below 0.5% of pricing-page traffic on enterprise-weighted traffic. Replace it with who it is for, three plain-language inclusions, the engagement model, one recognisable logo, and a threshold statement. The same spec is the fix when enterprise traffic arrives and no enterprise requests follow.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Page conversion to primary CTA | PLG or self-serve 3% to 8% typical, above 10% strong · sales-led to demo 2% to 5% typical, above 7% strong | Lintel guidance |
| Bounce rate | Above 60% on a self-serve page warrants investigation | Segment paid from organic before judging |
| Tier mix | No universal number. The anchor should take the largest share and match your segment sizing | Lintel guidance |
| Annual share of new business | 50%+ is a common target, lower for self-serve SMB | Annual saving below roughly 15% suppresses it |
| Discount rate by tier | Below 10% healthy for a researched price · sustained above 20% in one tier means the price or the contents are wrong | Lintel guidance |
| Price realization (net ÷ list) | 0.85 or above healthy · below 0.75 means list price is fiction | Lintel guidance |
| Post-purchase tier change (90 days) | Above 10% to 15% in either direction means buyers land in the wrong tier | Report each direction separately |
| Time on page | 60 to 180 seconds self-serve · under 30 sec they did not find what they needed · over 5 min they cannot decide | Lintel guidance |
| Scroll depth to comparison table | 30% to 50% when the table sits below the cards | Lintel guidance |
| Research samples | Van Westendorp 30+ per tier · choice-based conjoint 150+ respondents | Conjoint is not a seed-stage method |

**Where it goes wrong:** Packaging by feature inventory rather than buyer segment. Prices set by competitive benchmarking with no willingness-to-pay research. Three tiers with no deliberate anchor. A page written for the vendor instead of the buyer. Enterprise hidden behind "contact us" with no context about who it is for. Packaging that never changed after the product did. Treating the page as a one-time project instead of a standing cadence.

*Credit: Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter (Peter van Westendorp, 1976) · Gabor-Granger (André Gabor and Clive Granger, 1960s) · conjoint measurement in marketing (Paul Green and Vithala Rao, 1971) · willingness-to-pay conversations and the leader, filler, killer classification from Monetizing Innovation (Madhavan Ramanujam and Georg Tacke, 2016).*

## Competitive displacement and win-back  (FW-EMERGING-011)

Two motions aimed at buyers who already know the category: converting a competitor's customer, and re-acquiring one who churned. Includes defence when a competitor targets your base. Competitive research (FW-PM-004) and win/loss method (FW-PM-006) feed in here.

**When this matters:** Most target accounts already use a competitor and new logo growth has slowed for two or more quarters. You can name one provable advantage over one competitor for one segment, and at least three customers switched from them and stayed past 12 months. You have 25 or more churned accounts and a churn reason product or pricing has since fixed. A competitor just raised prices, was acquired, deprecated a feature, or had a public outage, opening a window that usually closes within one or two quarters.

**Skip it when:** First-time customers churn at or above your historical norm, so switchers leak out as fast as they arrive. Nobody can say why churned customers left. Differentiation is generic (easier, more flexible, better support) with no verifiable proof. Most of the market has no solution at all, so demand creation is cheaper. There is no field to tag an opportunity as displacement or win-back.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: wins happen, each handled differently, with no record of why any closed. Churned accounts untouched.
- Tier 2 Building: one written displacement thesis for the primary competitor from at least 10 competitive debriefs, and win-back sequences split by churn reason.
- Tier 3 Operating: trigger-based campaigns tied to monitored competitor events, and a scoped migration offering customer success signed off in writing.
- Tier 4 Compounding: competitor events on a 48-hour response clock, comparison content tested continuously, and win-back extended to former champions who changed employer.

**First build, in order**
1. Add four CRM fields: pipeline type (greenfield, displacement, win-back, expansion), competitor, churn reason, and confirmed-or-assumed. Do this before any campaign.
2. Reconstruct 12 months: incumbent, outcome, and a one-line reason per competitive deal, plus a churn reason on every lost account.
3. Run 10 rep debriefs and 5 buyer interviews, then write one thesis: segment, the dimension you beat them on, the proof.
4. Pick one motion, not both. Either contact the 10 to 20 churned accounts whose reason is genuinely fixed and that sit 6 to 12 months out, with a personal message naming the reason and the change. Or contact 20 to 30 accounts on the target competitor showing a trigger.
5. Publish one comparison page by use case with a migration section, dated sources, and legal review, routing its form fills straight to sales.
6. Scope the migration offering with written customer success sign-off and add a mandatory champion checkpoint before proposal. These two move late-stage win rate.

**Decision rules**
- Both motions wait if fit is the constraint. They move buyers into a product that does not hold them.
- If sales capacity is the constraint, restrict displacement to accounts scoring 6 or higher on displaceability. Switching deals consume 1.5 to 2.5 times the greenfield cycle.
- Require both segment fit and an active trigger before an account enters outbound.
- Win-back timing: the first 30 to 60 days are too early (honeymoon with the replacement) and after 18 to 24 months is usually too late (workflows rebuilt). For annual contracts the productive window is commonly 6 to 12 months, near the replacement vendor's first renewal.
- If a win-back campaign failed before, check three things in order: did the message name the churn reason, did the timing cohort match contract length, and was the root cause genuinely fixed at send time.
- If churn reasons shift toward service failure over two or more quarters, suspend win-back expansion. Service-failure churners recover slowest.
- If displacement customers churn more than 10 points above greenfield in the first 90 days, pause campaigns and audit migration delivery against what sales promised.
- If a rising share of competitive deals end in no decision rather than a competitor loss, shift spend from comparison content to risk reduction: pilots, shorter initial terms, migration guarantees, and references who made the same switch.
- If a qualified account cannot exit for 18 or more months, do not run a sales cycle. Convert to a relationship track and set an alert six months before renewal.
- Comparative claims must be truthful, substantiated, dated, and reviewed by counsel before publication.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Win rate against the named competitor | Below 25% is a real problem where you believe you compete · above 40% strong | Lintel guidance |
| Displacement cycle vs. greenfield | Commonly 1.5x to 2.5x · above 3x signals a specific blockage | Lintel guidance |
| Late-stage displacement win rate | Below 30% is a system problem, not bad luck | Usually champion politics or an incumbent counter-offer |
| Displacement share of new pipeline | 20% to 35% healthy in a maturing market · under 10% means it is not yet a channel | Lintel guidance |
| Discount and switcher churn | Discount within a few points of greenfield · 90-day switcher churn within 10 points of greenfield | Gaps mean price-led winning or under-delivered migration |
| Win-back re-activation | Roughly 18% on addressable product-gap accounts and 9% on price churners in one documented Tier 3 program | Use your own cohorts |
| Churn reason coverage | 90%+ tagged · below 60% makes win-back segmentation guesswork | Lintel guidance |
| Competitive debrief coverage | 50%+ · 60% to 70% strong for a manual program | Lintel guidance |
| Migration support load | 20 to 40 hours of delivery time per mid-market displacement deal | Usually binds before budget does |
| Enterprise win-back cycle | 12 to 18 months from first re-engagement | Staff it as a relationship, not a sequence |
| Budget and horizon | Series A 2% to 5% of marketing · Series B 3% to 8% · comparison page $2,000 to $5,000 · first results 60 to 90 days | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** Displacement built on price alone. No switching cost strategy, so deals stall after proposal. The wrong champion, so stakeholders nobody met kill the deal. Win-back that ignores the churn reason. Pipeline not tracked separately, so the motion cannot be funded. Emails to the same churned account at 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18 months with no response at any point.

## New market entry and launch  (FW-EMERGING-012)

Owns entering a new market or segment: which one next, the entry thesis, discovery before spend, the motion for the new buyers, the 90 days after launch, and the kill criteria. Running the launch itself is FW-PM-003. GTM motion design is FW-FOUND-005. International setup is FW-INTL-001 and FW-INTL-002.

**When this matters:** Leadership named a new segment and the plan so far is a launch date rather than a validated thesis. Growth is slowing from saturation (above roughly 50% of the reachable segment) rather than execution. A time-limited window closes within 6 to 12 months. Sales is already closing scattered deals outside the ICP and they concentrate in one recognizable segment. A launch is 90 days out with no post-launch plan, no kill criteria, and no reference customer in the segment.

**Skip it when:** The existing market is under roughly 30% penetrated and acquisition efficiency still improves with added spend. Expansion before consolidation is one of the most common causes of growth stall between $5M and $25M ARR. Also skip when unit economics are not repeatable at home, when the case rests on a few customer requests with no target-segment conversations, when no single owner can be named, or when resources can only come from people currently producing revenue.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: a named growth priority with no written thesis and no directly responsible individual.
- Tier 2 Building: a one-page thesis plus five to fifteen buyer conversations, then a written decision to go, narrow, or stop.
- Tier 3 Operating: a phased plan (pre-launch, launch window, 90-day post-launch) with owners, dates, leading indicators, and kill criteria on the same page as the success criteria.
- Tier 4 Compounding: entries run as a portfolio, reviewed quarterly, with resources moved toward entries beating target.

**First build, in order**
1. Write the thesis on one page: target segment, the specific problem, the alternatives those buyers use today, the differentiation claim, and five to ten named companies that should be early customers. Name a directly responsible individual.
2. Run five to fifteen conversations with target-segment buyers before any spend, then update, narrow, or abandon the thesis in writing.
3. Convert discovery participants into design partners or pilot customers. Launching with no segment reference costs 6 to 12 months of normal acquisition to fix.
4. Install the segment field on leads, contacts, and opportunities plus a cohort field on accounts. Without it the scaling decision cannot be made from data.
5. Write kill criteria alongside success criteria: which metric, at which threshold, by which date, triggers which review. Make it the condition of getting the investment approved.

**Decision rules**
- If fit is the constraint, do not run an entry. A second market spreads one diagnostic problem across two.
- If the existing market is under 30% penetrated, model the incremental revenue from the same resources at home first, and only revisit if that model is weaker than the discounted new-market case.
- If positioning is the constraint, sequence positioning first. A company that cannot say why it wins today writes a thesis that cannot be falsified.
- If someone calls customer requests proof a market exists, say requests are a thesis input, not validation, and ask for five conversations with non-customers.
- At pre-seed or seed on a first market, use absolute gates instead of core comparisons: five customers through a full usage cycle, 60% still actively using, and two willing to be named references.
- If moving upmarket, require discovery with procurement, security, and IT, and treat security attestations, single sign-on, contract terms, and service levels as launch prerequisites. Expect cycles 2 to 5 times current.
- If moving downmarket, verify cost to serve at the target price before any build and expect a self-serve layer. A direct sales motion carried downmarket is the most common cause of negative-margin entries.
- If partner-led, start recruitment 3 to 6 months before commercial launch and measure activated partners rather than signed ones.
- If regulated, confirm with counsel that you may lawfully serve the segment and add 3 to 9 months to every timeline.
- If first-cohort retention is below the core baseline for two consecutive periods, freeze scaling spend. Do not hire dedicated segment headcount beyond the owner until that retention has been measured.
- If someone asks about launch tactics and discovery is not done, answer the discovery question instead.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| ICP match rate of segment responders | 50%+ within 90 days of launch · below 30% means the ICP is too broad or targeting is off | Lintel guidance |
| Time to first segment opportunity | Under 30 days outbound-led · under 60 inbound-led · 90+ partner-led or category-creating | Lintel guidance |
| Pipeline coverage in the segment | 3x to 5x the revenue target, in line with core coverage | The conversion assumptions come from the core market |
| Meeting-to-opportunity and close rate | Within 20% of the core rate by day 120 · core close rates commonly 40% to 60% | Lintel guidance |
| Sales cycle vs. core | Upmarket 2x to 5x · adjacent vertical 1.2x to 1.6x in year one, converging after | Lintel guidance |
| First-cohort 90-day retention | At or above the core market's 90-day retention | This is the scaling gate |
| Time to first value vs. core | At or below core · above 1.5x core predicts retention problems before retention data exists | Lintel guidance |
| Segment CAC and payback | At or below 1.5x core CAC at 180 days · payback under 12 months strong, above 24 structural | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** A thesis built on assumptions rather than buyer evidence. The wrong motion carried over from the core market. Premature scaling. Launch-moment bias, where the announcement gets the attention and the post-launch 90 days get neither owner nor plan. Resource cannibalization. No kill criteria, treated as pessimism instead of governance. Launching with no reference customer in the segment.

## Conversational marketing: chat, SMS, and voice  (FW-EMERGING-016)

Real-time two-way conversation as a pipeline system: website chat, meeting booking and routing, SMS, and voice including AI agents. Being the answer inside AI search surfaces is FW-SEO-007. The human SDR team is FW-SALES-002.

**When this matters:** High-intent pages get steady traffic but under 2% to 3% become a lead or booked meeting. Median time to first response is longer than an hour, or after-hours leads wait until the next day. Buyers repeatedly ask the same 10 to 20 pre-sales questions. Inbound calls go to voicemail and nobody counts the misses. 30% or more of booked meetings no-show or are unqualified.

**Skip it when:** High-intent pages get under roughly 1,000 monthly visitors, so demand creation is the constraint. Nobody can staff conversations or maintain an AI agent's knowledge, and a wrong answer would hurt trust more than no chat. Buyers are enterprise committees arriving through outbound or events. You are regulated with no consent capture, disclosure, or recordkeeping for texts and calls.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: a widget greeting everyone the same way, unstaffed or bot-answered, not connected to routing.
- Tier 2 Building: qualification-first chat on pricing and demo pages only, with 3 to 4 questions routing qualified visitors to instant booking.
- Tier 3 Operating: an AI agent on an approved knowledge base for after-hours and first-line conversations, with handoff rules and weekly transcript review.
- Tier 4 Compounding: chat, SMS, and voice share one contact record, intent signals trigger proactive conversations, and outcomes feed attribution and retraining.

**First build, in order**
1. Measure median speed to first response and count missed calls and unanswered chats last month.
2. Put a booking calendar directly on the demo-request confirmation page. Cheapest win available.
3. Launch qualification-first chat on pricing and demo pages only, with 3 to 4 questions.
4. Route qualified visitors to instant booking, keeping calendar availability inside 3 business days.
5. Add SMS and email reminders, which commonly cut no-shows by a third or more. Only then add an AI agent, with handoff rules and weekly transcript review.

**Decision rules**
- Target under 5 minutes to first response. Under 1 minute is excellent, over an hour is failing.
- If chat conversion is healthy but booking is low, the friction is the calendar, not the conversation.
- If meeting-held rate is below 50% to 70%, check whether routing sends buyers to a queue and whether availability is more than 3 business days out.
- If qualified-conversation rate is above 35%, your criteria may be too loose. Review meeting outcomes before celebrating.
- If AI usage costs grow faster than qualified meetings, the agent is answering the wrong conversations.
- Buy chat and AI agents rather than building. Own live coverage internally for target accounts and outsource overflow or after-hours.
- Never launch SMS or AI voice in a regulated space before consent capture, disclosure, and recordkeeping exist.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Median speed to first response | Under 5 min strong · under 1 min excellent · over 1 hr failing | Lintel guidance |
| High-intent page to booked meeting | 1% to 3% with forms only · 3% to 8% with qualification-first chat and instant booking | Lintel guidance |
| Chat engagement rate | 1% to 5% site-wide · 5% to 15% on pricing pages with targeted greetings | Lintel guidance |
| Qualified conversation rate | 15% to 35% on high-intent pages | Lintel guidance |
| Conversation to booked meeting | 50% to 70% | Lintel guidance |
| Meeting-held and meeting-to-opportunity | 75% to 85% held · 40% to 60% to opportunity | Reminders cut no-shows by a third or more |
| Conversational share of inbound pipeline | 10% to 30% for mature B2B programs | Lintel guidance |
| Cost per qualified meeting | Often $75 to $250 mid-market, against $200 to $600+ from paid channels | Lintel guidance |
| Unit costs | AI resolution roughly $0.50 to $2 each · AI voice $0.05 to $0.30/min plus telephony · US SMS roughly $0.01 to $0.05/message | 2026 reference, verify current pricing |
| Budget and coverage | Series A 3% to 8% of marketing · Series B 4% to 10% · roughly 1 SDR hour of chat coverage per 50 to 100 high-intent conversations a week | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** A greeter widget that treats every visitor the same. A qualified buyer left waiting. An AI agent that invents answers. An after-hours black hole. Texting and calling without consent. An interrogation flow that asks too much before giving anything. Measuring conversation volume instead of revenue.

## Out of home and experiential  (FW-EMERGING-015)

**When this matters:** Your buyers cluster in a geography you can name (a metro, a corridor, a set of office buildings, a single conference) and one placement reaches a large share of them. You are trying to become the default name and losing deals to familiarity. You already have a working demand-capture engine. A high-stakes moment concentrates your buyers in one place and time.

**Skip it when:** Pre-fit. The capture layer is weak, so someone who searches your name after seeing the placement lands nowhere. Buyers are scattered with no nameable cluster. You cannot fund enough reach and frequency to register: one billboard for two weeks in a large market is below the threshold where memory forms. You cannot instrument branded search, direct traffic, or a geo holdout.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: a one-off billboard or a conference booth, with no measurement design.
- Tier 2 Building: one contained, measurable placement (a conference activation or a geofenced digital out-of-home flight around your buyers' offices) with a pre-set holdout or baseline.
- Tier 3 Operating: a repeatable playbook tying every placement to a lift measurement and a capture path.
- Tier 4 Compounding: always-on programmatic digital out-of-home keyed to account and intent signals, with brand-lift and incrementality testing feeding a reach-and-frequency model.

**First build, in order**
1. Name the single tightest geography or moment where your buyers are physically concentrated.
2. Confirm you can measure branded search and direct traffic before spending anything.
3. Design the measurement before launch: a geo holdout, or a clean before-and-after baseline.
4. Run one contained placement at enough frequency to register, keeping the capture path live for the whole flight.
5. Read branded search lift, direct traffic lift, and incremental pipeline, then compute cost per incremental opportunity against other channels.

**Decision rules**
- Plan frequency of 3 to 5 within a burst flight, or 1 to 2 per week continuous. High reach at frequency 1 to 2 rarely builds memory, and above 10 in a 4-week flight is wear-out.
- Judge effective CPM against the best digital channel reaching the same buyers, which often runs $100 to $400 for narrow B2B audiences once waste is counted.
- If branded search lift is under 5%, it is not distinguishable from noise and the placement was under-scaled.
- If search lift appears but inbound does not, you have presence without capture. Fix the landing experience before buying more media.
- Plan 1 to 2 times the sponsorship fee in activation spend. An unactivated sponsorship is a logo.
- Buy media through self-serve or marketplace platforms below roughly $250,000 a year, outsource above that, and always own measurement internally.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Reach in the target geography | 50% to 70% for a well-planned flight · above 70% usually needs multiple formats | Lintel guidance |
| Frequency | 3 to 5 within a burst · 1 to 2 per week continuous · above 10 in 4 weeks is wear-out | Lintel guidance |
| CPM | Static roadside roughly $3 to $10 in most US markets, $15 to $30+ premium urban cores · programmatic DOOH $5 to $20 open, $15 to $50 premium place-based | Verify current rates |
| Branded search and direct traffic lift | Branded search 10% to 30% net for a well-scaled flight (under 5% is noise) · direct traffic 5% to 20% | Lintel guidance |
| Incremental pipeline lift | 5% to 15% for integrated B2B programs | Confidence intervals are wide at low volume |
| Awareness lift | Aided 3 to 10 points for a well-scaled campaign (under 2 is inside the margin of error) · unaided 1 to 5 points over 6 to 12 months | Single flights rarely move unaided |
| Account-level DOOH | 1.2x to 2.0x engagement vs. matched unexposed accounts · outbound reply lift 10% to 40% relative | Sequences must reference the local presence |
| Unit costs | Booth space roughly $20 to $60 per square foot at US B2B shows, total exhibit cost about 3x floor space · static billboard $750 to $3,000/mo small markets, $5,000 to $30,000+ premium top-10 · airport $5,000 to $50,000/mo | Verify current rates |
| Budget and return | Seed 0% to 10% of marketing (events only) · Series A 5% to 15% · Series B 10% to 25% · pipeline-to-spend 3:1 to 8:1 within 2 quarters · cost per qualified conversation $150 to $500 | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** The under-scaled placement. Audience-to-geography mismatch, where frequency is fine but the people are elsewhere. Presence without capture. Measurement designed after the fact. Vanity-driven placement selection. An activation with no reason for anyone to participate. Creative built for the wrong medium, usually too many words for a 3-second read.

## International expansion, localization, and in-market demand  (FW-INTL-001, FW-INTL-002, FW-INTL-003)

Three stages in order. FW-INTL-001 decides which markets and in what order and owns the readiness score. FW-INTL-002 adapts product, messaging, content, and motion so a market feels native. FW-INTL-003 fills the pipeline there. Do not run these out of order: demand generation in a market whose localization is below Tier 2 produces high-CAC pipeline that does not close at home rates.

**When this matters:** You closed 2 to 3 deals in one international market with no dedicated marketing, so the market pulls before you push. Leadership committed to an international target in 12 months with no strategy. A rep or country manager was hired with no supporting infrastructure. For localization: conversion or close rates abroad are materially below home and you ruled out product and pricing.

**Skip it when:** Home fit is not clearly established, because international amplifies problems. The home market has significant unpenetrated audience. You cannot bill in local currency, comply with local data rules, or support local time zones. Minimum $50K to $150K per market in year one at Series A is not committed. There is no clear owner. For demand generation: the market has not produced 10 or more qualified opportunities through any mechanism.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: no market selection work. Foreign inbound is closed opportunistically and never analyzed.
- Tier 2 Building: one proof-of-concept market defined explicitly (one country, one ICP segment, one GTM motion, one 6-month success metric), a native-speaker review workflow, an approved brand glossary, and one channel matching the dominant buyer discovery pattern.
- Tier 3 Operating: regional infrastructure (localized positioning, a regional demand program, a local partner or agency, a named owner), a translation management system with a quality scorecard, and market-specific pipeline reporting.
- Tier 4 Compounding: a repeatable entry playbook applied to the next market, transcreation for the highest-impact content, and local campaign calendars with local budget authority.

**First build, in order**
1. Run the demand signal audit. Which countries are current customers coming from without you marketing there? It is a data pull and a spreadsheet, and it needs no marketing expertise.
2. Score each candidate market on the Market Entry Readiness Score (MERS): demand signal, operational feasibility, ICP validity, the competition in that market, GTM motion clarity, and marketing infrastructure readiness. Each dimension is 4 points, for a total of 6 to 24. Allow 2 to 3 hours per market.
3. Pick one market and define it as one country, one ICP segment, one GTM motion, and one 6-month success metric.
4. Inventory the localization surface area (every customer-facing asset that would need adapting) before any translation work.
5. Build the minimum viable marketing infrastructure before or alongside the first sales hire. It takes 4 to 8 weeks and costs less than one month of a rep's salary.
6. Map how every international deal closed in the last 12 months, launch one channel matching that pattern, and run it 90 days before adding a second.
7. Produce at least 2 local case studies before scaling demand generation, and report CAC, stage conversion, and channel contribution separately per market.

**Decision rules**
- MERS 6 to 10: not ready, fix the two lowest dimensions on a 6-month plan and rescore. 11 to 14: conditionally ready, enter with a 90-day gap resolution plan. 15 to 18: ready, expect a 6 to 9 month ramp. 19 to 24: accelerate.
- At seed, advise against deliberate international investment. The exception is an inherently global product (developer tool, API, horizontal SaaS) where organic adoption is already happening. Close those deals as signals and spend nothing.
- At Series A with 3 or more customers from one market acquired without marketing, score it. Above 14, a proof-of-concept program is justified at $50K to $100K for year one, covered by a home-market rep before hiring locally.
- Below Series C, never enter multiple markets at once. Simultaneous entry spreads investment below the viable threshold everywhere.
- If the target is Japan, Germany, or Southeast Asia, the default motion is partner-mediated. A direct-only motion there produces long cycles and low close rates no matter how good the demand program is.
- If PLG, the entry question is product readiness: local currency, local payment methods, local-language UI, and no data-compliance blocker on self-serve signup.
- Never hire a rep in a market before the minimum marketing infrastructure exists. A rep hired into an empty market spends six months doing market development marketing should have done.
- Do not localize while still validating whether the opportunity is real. A US company entering Canada or the UK may need only light adaptation.
- If 12 or more months in and pipeline is below the success metric, diagnose before continuing or exiting. The three common causes are ICP mismatch, wrong GTM motion, and missing local credibility.
- No single channel should produce more than 60% of pipeline in a market. At Tier 3, three to four channels should each contribute 15% to 35%.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| MERS | 6 to 10 not ready · 11 to 14 conditional · 15 to 18 ready · 19 to 24 accelerate · target 3 to 5 points improvement per 6 months under preparation | Lintel guidance |
| International share of pipeline | Series A 1 market 5% to 10% · Series B 1 to 2 markets 10% to 20% · Series C+ 3+ markets 20% to 35% | Lintel guidance |
| International CAC vs. home | Months 1 to 6 2x to 4x · months 12 to 18 1.5x to 2.5x · months 24 to 36 approaching 1x to 1.5x | Year 3+ should be within 1.3x |
| Cost per qualified opportunity | Early entry $3,000 to $8,000 · established market (months 12 to 24) $800 to $2,000 | Market development cost is embedded early |
| New market MQLs and conversion | 5 to 20 MQLs a month in months 1 to 6, SQL conversion at 40% to 50% of the home rate while the team learns · within 15% of home once established | Lintel guidance |
| Partner-sourced share in partner-mediated markets | 30% to 50% within 12 months of partner activation · hybrid markets 20% to 35% | Lintel guidance |
| Sales cycle vs. home | 30% to 60% longer in months 1 to 12 · 10% to 30% longer at months 18 to 36 · German and Japanese cycles may run 20% to 40% longer | Lintel guidance |
| Conversion gap after localization | Within 10% to 15% of home at each stage · gaps above 25% at a stage point to a localization problem there | Lintel guidance |
| Local case studies | Minimum 2 before scaled demand generation · 2 to 4 per market per year after · enterprise needs 4 to 6 per major market and vertical | Lintel guidance |
| Localization coverage | Highest-priority content 100% coverage and 95% fresh, second tier 80%, third 50% · turnaround 5 business days top tier, 30 days second | Lintel guidance |
| Translation cost | $0.12 to $0.25 per word for professional human B2B software translation, declining as translation memory grows | Mature memory utilization 25% to 40% |
| Outbound reply rates by market | US and UK 3% to 6% for well-crafted sequences · Germany 2% to 4% · Japan 1% to 3% cold | Lintel guidance |
| Paid cost per lead vs. home | Typically 20% to 50% above home for comparable ICP targeting on LinkedIn | Lintel guidance |
| Localized SEO ramp | 9 to 18 months to meaningful organic traffic | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** Market selection based on anecdote rather than demand data. Treating expansion as a translation problem, which is the easiest part. The wrong motion for the market. Simultaneous multi-market entry. No local credibility, so buyers have no reference like themselves. No success metric defined before entry. Marketing hired after sales. Orphaned translations that drift from updated home content. Regional pipeline buried inside a global number.
