# Marketing operations, tools, and hiring

How to run the machinery around a B2B marketing team: which tools to buy and cut, how to work with agencies and freelancers, when to use them instead of staff, and who to hire first at each stage. For marketers and founders at seed to Series B software companies.

**Owns:** Which marketing tools to keep, cut, or buy, and how to evaluate them. The stack audit, utilization thresholds, and stack health score. The brief, SOW, review, and renewal workflow for outside vendors. Agency versus freelancer versus fractional versus in-house. Hiring process, onboarding, development, and retention for marketers. What to hire first at each stage.
**Does not own:** Which tool categories the stack needs, choosing the CRM, how tools connect, build order (FW-FOUND-007). MAP and CRM sync, UTM and lead-source tracking, enrichment, lead scoring, routing (FW-REVOPS-002). Email consent, privacy, claims substantiation, and other compliance (FW-OPS-002). The measurement model and attribution (FW-FOUND-006). Choosing the GTM motion (FW-FOUND-005) and the annual marketing plan (FW-FOUND-009). The ICP, positioning, messaging, and voice documents that vendors and hires are briefed from (FW-FOUND-001, FW-FOUND-003, FW-FOUND-004, FW-BRAND-003). The MQL handoff to sales (FW-REVOPS-001, FW-SALES-007). Pricing and packaging. Employment law, severance, and contract law: get a lawyer or HR professional.

**Routing across the three tool frameworks:** confirm the category is needed (FW-FOUND-007), pick and govern the vendor here, then set it up (FW-REVOPS-002).

## The marketing tool stack: audit, cut, buy, renew  (FW-OPS-003)

**When this matters:**
- You are about to buy a tool above $500/month on the strength of a demo and a peer recommendation.
- The stack has 10 or more tools and nobody can say what each does, who uses it, or when it renews.
- A new marketing leader inherited a stack they did not pick.
- Two tools do the same job and you pay for both.
- The person running marketing operations spends more than 20% of their time on tool administration.

**Skip it when:**
- The stack has fewer than five tools and every one is used and paying off.
- You are pre-product-market fit and the decisions are the basics (CRM, email, analytics). Install an inventory and a renewal calendar and stop there.
- A major tool was bought in the last 12 months and is still being adopted. Measuring it early gives bad data.
- A platform migration, an end-of-quarter push, a product launch, or a leadership change is underway. Audits and swaps in those windows add risk and little gain.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: Tools are bought when someone feels a need. No inventory, no owner, renewals happen on their own, total cost is only knowable by reading card statements (some subscriptions sit on personal cards).
- Tier 2 Building: Inventory exists, one named owner, a purchase check for new tools, quarterly utilization review on bigger tools, a stack map, and some overlap already removed.
- Tier 3 Operating: A scoring rubric for any tool above $500/month, total cost of ownership (TCO) for tools above $1,000/month, monitored integrations, a renewal review 90 days before each major contract, and a 12-month tool roadmap.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Return on technology investment is calculated each year. Every tool has a written reason, an alternative considered, and a cost-to-value ratio reviewed in the past 12 months.

**First build, in order**
1. **Audit every subscription (3 to 5 hours).** Pull from the budget, corporate and personal card statements, app-store billing, and a chat with every person on the team. Record for each tool: name, what it is for, monthly or annual cost, renewal date, active users, and a utilization rating from 1 to 5 (3 means used regularly for its main purpose). Include the $40 tools. The audit is not done until every dollar of software spend, on or off budget, is accounted for.
2. **Cancel the obvious waste.** Cancel (right away if month-to-month, at renewal if annual) any tool where no one has logged in for 60 days, another tool already does the job, or the person who bought it has left and nobody can explain it. If you are unsure, confirm the use case first. One extra month of a subscription costs less than cutting something a person relies on.
3. **Apply the utilization rule.** Review any tool below 50% utilization. Try one fix (training or reconfiguring). If it is still below 30%, cancel at the next renewal.
4. **Set a renewal calendar (1 hour).** For every tool above $200/month, set an alert 90 days before renewal labeled "renewal decision required." Without it, renewals keep happening by default.
5. **Add a purchase gate (30 minutes).** Any new tool above $200/month needs the marketing lead's approval after a 15-minute check that the inventory has no tool doing the same job.
6. **Move on when you can answer from memory:** what each tool does, what it costs, when it renews, and who uses it. Expect 4 to 6 weeks after the audit.
7. **Then add structure.** Name a stack owner. Build the stack map (a table with an integrations column is enough). Write a one-page needs assessment template: problem, how it is handled today, monthly cost of the gap, success definition, alternatives (including non-tool ones). No tool above $200/month moves to evaluation without one.
8. **Evaluate purchases above $500/month with the rubric below.** Score at least two candidates (three above $1,000/month). Run a hands-on trial in your own environment with your own data, not a vendor-run sandbox. Get two references you found yourself, not only the vendor's list.
9. **Write the implementation plan before you sign.** Timeline, hours needed from whom, integration work, training, expected active users at 30, 60, and 90 days, and one named adoption owner. Review at 30 and 90 days.
10. **Run the renewal process 90 days out** for tools above $500/month on annual contracts: utilization review, value review (did it produce the outcome it was bought for), a 30-minute market check, then renew, renegotiate, downgrade, or exit. Above $2,000/month, write one paragraph justifying the renewal.
11. **Calculate TCO** for tools above $1,000/month: subscription, plus implementation (spread over the contract term), plus integration upkeep, plus admin time, plus training.

**Tool evaluation rubric (for a purchase).** Six dimensions, each scored 1 to 5, total 6 to 30:
| Dimension | What it asks |
|---|---|
| Problem fit | Does it solve the problem written in the needs assessment? |
| Integration fit | Native, two-way data flow with the tools it must connect to? |
| Stage and team fit | Right-sized for your stage, team, and data? |
| Cost efficiency | Is value above total cost at realistic utilization? |
| Vendor quality | Stable, supported, likely to exist for the contract term? |
| Adoption likelihood | Will the team use it at the needed level? |

Read the total: 25 to 30 proceed and document why. 19 to 24 viable, fix the lowest scores first and negotiate an exit if adoption targets are missed. 13 to 18 marginal, check whether another tool scores higher. 6 to 12 do not buy.

**Stack health scorecard (for the existing stack).** Five dimensions scored 1 to 4, total 5 to 20, run quarterly: coverage (gaps needing manual work), utilization (4 means 85% or more of tools rate 3+; 3 means 70 to 85%; 2 means 50 to 70%; 1 means under 50%), integration health, cost efficiency, and governance (owner, inventory, renewal calendar, purchase gate). Read the total: 17 to 20 healthy, keep the cadence. 13 to 16 sound, fix the lowest dimension within 90 days. 9 to 12 real problems, at least one dimension needs attention now. 5 to 8 run a full audit and repair program.

**Decision rules**
- If you are a founder-led seed company with no marketing hire and stack cost is above $500/month, then audit now and keep only a CRM (free tier is fine), an email tool (free tier is fine), and Google Analytics. Anything else needs a documented use case that free tools cannot serve.
- If the tool is for a need you expect in 18 to 24 months, then do not buy it. It will auto-renew twice before you use it, and a better, cheaper one will exist later.
- If you are a new marketing leader inheriting a stack, then days 1 to 30 are inventory only, day 30 present the health score, day 60 make cancellation calls, day 90 make the first additions. This stops you from cutting something someone silently depends on or buying what already exists.
- If a tool has no native CRM integration, then treat it as a primary risk and add the integration cost (custom work or an iPaaS such as Zapier, Make, or Workato) to its TCO. Pick the natively integrated alternative unless the other tool fits the problem far better.
- If the stack is many tools with few connections, then fix integration before adding anything. Zapier suits simple cases, Make or Workato complex ones.
- If you must cut cost fast, then do four things in order: cancel tools still under 30% after a fix attempt, downgrade each tool to the lowest tier covering the features in use, drop one of any overlapping pair (keep the higher-utilization one), and ask for a reduction on your three biggest annual contracts (Lintel guidance: long-standing customers often get 10 to 20% when they ask). Never cancel the CRM, marketing automation, or analytics to save money.
- If two tools overlap, then compare active users in the last 30 days, features in real use, and team preference. Cancel the lower one unless it covers a use the other cannot. Do not consolidate during a campaign launch.
- If a new tool misses its 30-day adoption target, then intervene within 10 business days. Diagnose workflow friction (rebuild the process around the tool), a skill gap (targeted training), or a poor fit (check your contract for an early exit).
- If you are a single marketer, then lean toward an all-in-one platform (HubSpot is the library's example) over best-of-breed. Fewer integrations and one place to administer matter more than depth.
- If your motion is product-led and messages trigger on in-product behavior, then look at product-event tools (Customer.io, Vero, Braze) before a traditional marketing automation platform.
- If you are choosing a CRM, marketing automation, or BI tool, then compare three vendors, weight integration fit and stage fit most, and get two peer references at your stage and motion. Do not default to the category leader.
- If someone proposes a customer data platform, then require a use case your CRM and automation cannot serve, enough data volume, and engineering time. Try an iPaaS or reverse-ETL tool (Census, Hightouch) first.
- If a founder or executive mandates a tool, then score it honestly. A poor score goes back with the specific gaps, plus an offer of a 90-day pilot with success criteria.
- If a vendor is acquired or shuts down, then start exporting data at once and score replacements with the rubric.
- If you are replacing a major platform (for example moving from Mailchimp to full automation), then avoid your busiest demand quarter, run old and new together for 60 to 90 days, and set a hard cutover date and a rollback condition.
- If you are in a regulated industry (healthcare, financial services, legal), then compliance gates come first (a BAA for health data, SOC 2 Type II, SSO, role controls, data deletion). A tool without them is out regardless of its score. FW-OPS-002 owns the detail.
- If you are not a marketer, then buy no tool above $500/month on a demo alone.
- If the stack looks expensive for what it produces, then it is usually a utilization problem. Audit before you cut by feel.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Tools by stage | Seed 4-8 (10+ is sprawl). Series A 8-15. Series B 15-25. Series C+ 20-35 | Lintel guidance |
| Typical monthly stack cost | Seed $0-200. Series A $2,000-6,000. Series B $8,000-20,000 | Lintel guidance; seed minimum viable stack is CRM, email, Google Analytics, LinkedIn |
| Tool spend as share of marketing budget | Roughly 15-25% at any stage. Above about 25%, run a consolidation audit within 30 days | Lintel guidance; no reliable survey splits this by stage |
| Stack utilization rate (tools rated 3+ of 5) | 80% or more. Below 70% is significant waste, audit within 90 days | Lintel guidance |
| Stack health score | 17+ healthy. Below 13 means real problems | Lintel guidance |
| Tools per marketer | 2-4 manageable. Above 5 likely exceeds capacity | Lintel guidance |
| Critical integrations working | 100% (CRM to automation, analytics to BI). Fix a failure within 24 hours, root cause in 5 business days | Secondary integrations below 90% get repair priority |
| Renewal decision timing | Decide within 30 days of the 90-day alert. A decision within 14 days of renewal is an auto-renewal in practice | Lintel guidance |
| New tools hitting 90-day adoption targets | 80% or more. Below 60% is a process problem | Lintel guidance |
| Review process compliance | 90% or more of purchases above $500/month. Below 80% means the process is too heavy or unenforced | Lintel guidance |
| Out-of-cycle trigger | Active users drop over 30% month over month on a tool above $1,000/month | Lintel guidance |
| TCO multipliers | Foundation tools: implementation about 2-4x first-year subscription, admin about 15-25% of subscription per year. Specialist tools: implementation about 0.5-1x | Lintel guidance, mid-market |
| When to hire marketing ops | Stack above $8,000/month, more than 15 tools, steady data-quality problems from integration gaps, marketing lead spending over 5 hours a week on administration | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:**
- You judge a tool by its features and skip the integration check, then find a custom engineering project nobody scoped.
- The vendor runs your trial in a clean sandbox. Real data and real messiness show up only after you sign.
- No utilization target is written at purchase, so any usage level gets called fine at renewal.
- The tool outruns the team's skills (an advanced SEO suite with nobody who can act on it).
- You negotiate only when you are ready to leave. Check alternatives 90 days out every time, so you negotiate with a real option.
- You build the scoring process on top of a dirty stack, or cancel too fast and break a workflow someone relied on.

## Working with agencies, freelancers, and consultants: briefs, SOWs, reviews, renewals  (FW-OPS-001)

**When this matters:**
- You spend $50,000 a year or more on outside help with no review process or quality standard.
- Output quality swings between projects and you cannot tell if the cause is the brief, the vendor, or your own feedback.
- One vendor is a dependency: if they left tomorrow, you would lose capability or history.
- Your team spends more than 20% of its time managing vendors.
- You are about to hire your first full-time marketer and have two or more agencies.

**Skip it when:**
- You have no outside vendors and no plan for any.
- Total outside spend is under $15,000 a year, one person manages it, and quality is fine.
- You are pre-product-market fit, or a strategy pivot will change which vendor skills you need.
- The founders have deep marketing skill and do the work themselves. Build the core system first.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: Vendors arrived by referral or trial, no central list, assignments given verbally, review is by gut feel, contracts renew by default.
- Tier 2 Building: Briefs are written and approved before work starts, contracts set scope and basic IP terms, each vendor has one internal owner, review uses a checklist, and you have made at least one renewal decision on purpose.
- Tier 3 Operating: Vendors are sorted into strategic partners, preferred vendors, and transactional vendors. Quarterly business reviews (QBRs) and a scorecard run for the big ones. Contracts require knowledge hand-over. Make-versus-buy is explicit.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Rates are benchmarked each year, at least one strategic partner is paid partly on outcomes, partners join annual planning, and outside spend is a tracked share of the marketing budget.

**First build, in order**
1. **Inventory every vendor (1 to 2 hours).** For each: name, type (agency, freelancer, fractional, consultant, platform), one-sentence scope, monthly cost, contract status and end date, internal owner, one-line satisfaction note. Do this before anything else.
2. **Write a one-page brief template (2 to 3 hours).** Eight fields: deliverable and format, objective in business terms, audience (ICP company profile and buyer), core message or claim, tone and voice, channel and format needs, strong examples, and success criteria. Make it mandatory. A template that is optional is not a system. Run the first three briefs yourself, then adjust.
3. **Close the worst contract gap first (2 to 4 hours).** For any vendor above $1,000/month, confirm in writing that you own the work product and that you own and can access every ad account, pixel, and audience. Ask for an addendum if not. These two terms cause the worst damage when a vendor leaves badly. Do not rewrite every contract at once.
4. **You are out of zero-to-one when:** the inventory is current, briefs are used on all major deliverables, and IP and account access are covered for every vendor above $1,000/month.
5. **Write a standard SOW (statement of work).** Cover: parties, term, scope in countable units (eight social assets a month, not "social support"), quality standards, revision limit (typically two to three rounds) with a change order process, fees, IP as work-for-hire owned by you on payment, account and data access handed back within 48 hours of termination, confidentiality, termination notice (30 days for retainers under $10,000/month, 60 for bigger) with a knowledge-transfer duty, and performance standards (required for strategic partners). Have a lawyer review the template once.
6. **Onboard each vendor in writing before the first job.** Give them: company overview, ICP and buyer profiles, messaging, competitive positioning, voice guide, examples that worked and examples that missed, and the brief and rubric. End with a small paid test brief and score it before the full engagement starts.
7. **Add a review rubric to every brief.** Four or five criteria scored 1 to 5 (for an ad: ICP match, claim specificity, voice, format). Any first round scoring below 12 of 20 goes back to the vendor before you spend time giving feedback.
8. **Set the cadence.** Weekly: 30 minutes or an async update on work in progress. Monthly: rubric scores, scope versus actual, change orders. Quarterly: full scorecard, market-rate check, and a keep, restructure, or end call. Spend above $5,000/month also gets a QBR with an agenda and a written summary.
9. **Run a renewal review 90 days before every contract ends:** average quality score, a rate check against at least two alternatives, a make-versus-buy recheck, scope fit, then a decision. A renewal without this review is a default, not a decision.
10. **Hand the exit protocol to the vendor on the day you give notice:** campaign history, targeting rationale, account access, creative assets, process notes, and an overlap period with the new provider.

**Vendor scorecard (any vendor above $2,500/month).** Five dimensions scored 1 to 5, total 5 to 25: output quality, brief adherence, reliability and timeliness, strategic alignment, commercial value. Read the total: 22 to 25 strategic partner, invest and share context. 17 to 21 preferred, fix the weakest dimension. 11 to 16 watch list, hold a formal performance talk, set a 60-day threshold, and start looking at alternatives. 5 to 10 underperforming, start a formal improvement plan or move out, and add no scope.

**Portfolio health score (the whole system).** Seven dimensions scored 0 to 3, total 21: inventory, briefing discipline, contract standards, performance reviews, make-versus-buy policy, dependency and redundancy, market-rate benchmarking. 19 to 21 optimized. 14 to 18 managed, raise any dimension under 2. 8 to 13 developing, fix briefs and contracts first. 0 to 7 ad hoc, do inventory and basic briefing before any performance management.

**Decision rules**
- If you are a founder-led seed company on a minimal budget, then do two things only: a brief for every assignment, and a written agreement on IP and account access for every vendor above $500/month. Skip scorecards and QBRs until outside spend passes $5,000/month.
- If you have not written an ICP, positioning, and messaging, then do not sign a recurring retainer. A one-off project (a brand sprint, a positioning workshop, a website build) is fine. No brief template can make up for missing inputs.
- If you hear "great to work with, but no results," then separate three causes: the strategy behind the brief is wrong, the work looks professional but misses your standard, or attribution is not capturing the vendor's effect. If quality scores well and pipeline is low, fix the brief before you replace the vendor.
- If the vendor "isn't getting our brand" after 90 days, then check your onboarding first. Re-brief if the ICP or messaging changed since. Only a current onboarding plus a still-generic result is a real alignment problem.
- If quality swings month to month, then check account team turnover, brief quality by month, and whether several of your people give conflicting feedback. Name one internal owner who gives the final feedback.
- If a vendor is doing well and you want to expand scope, then first make sure the current scope is documented well enough to brief a replacement. If the function is one you plan to bring in-house, add knowledge-transfer duties to the expansion.
- If you plan to end a vendor and fear losing history, then start the exit protocol now. If the contract has no hand-over clause, offer to pay the full notice period in exchange for the documentation. If they refuse, write down campaign history, access, audience data, and process notes yourself before you give notice.
- If you run a sales-led motion with an automation platform and an agency over $5,000/month, then require proven experience with your platform and brief each job by funnel stage with a conversion target.
- If you run a product-led motion, then brief in one-to-two-week cycles with a set number of variants per cycle and a performance threshold that triggers rotation. Monthly-campaign agencies tend to fit poorly.
- If you are in a regulated industry, then put a compliance review window (typically 5 to 10 business days) into the SOW as a normal stage, and require the agency to cite sources for factual claims. FW-OPS-002 owns the rules.
- If you are a solo marketer spending over 20% of your time on vendors, then check for missing briefs, tolerated underperformers, and scope that no longer matches the need.
- If you are not a marketer, then judge output, not relationship. Ask: of the last five deliverables, how many needed one round, arrived on time, and sounded like your company? A pleasant, responsive agency can still produce mediocre work.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| First-round approval rate | Above 60% for relationships of 18+ months. Above 40% in the first 90 days | Lintel guidance |
| Average revision rounds | 1.2-1.8 established. Under 2.5 in the first six months. Sustained above 2.5 needs action | Lintel guidance |
| On-time delivery | Above 85% established. Above 75% in the first three months | Lintel guidance |
| Change-order rate | Under 15% of months. Above 20% for two quarters means restructure the SOW | Lintel guidance |
| Composite scorecard | Below 16 for two quarters means improvement plan or transition | Lintel guidance |
| Brief adherence and alignment scores | Adherence 4+ after six months. Alignment 3+ (4+ for strategic partners) | Lintel guidance |
| Account team continuity | Above 75% over 12 months. Any unannounced team change triggers a QBR | Lintel guidance |
| Rate versus market | Within 15% either way is fine for a performing vendor. Above a 20% premium with no clear quality reason means renegotiate. A rate increase request above 10% triggers a check | Lintel guidance |
| Internal time to manage a vendor | A $10,000/month retainer should take no more than about 15-20 internal hours a month. A founder should spend no more than 2 hours a week on any one vendor | Lintel guidance |
| Number of vendors one marketer can manage | 2-3 well. Four or more usually costs more in overhead than it adds | Lintel guidance |
| Renewal reviews done on time | 100% of renewals above $2,500/month | Lintel guidance |
| Knowledge transfer completed at exit | 100% | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:**
- Brief-free relationship: no written brief, so every job takes extra revision rounds. Revisions are used to do the briefing.
- Scope creep without change orders: work and invoices drift above the contract and nobody records it.
- Invisible dependency: you assume the vendor would hand everything over if asked, but the contract never required it and you do not hold the accounts.
- Comfort renewals and performance tolerance: the team complains about a vendor in meetings, nothing changes, and the contract renews anyway.
- Context starvation and drift: onboarding was done once, then the ICP or messaging changed and the vendor was never re-briefed.
- Activity reports taken as performance: posts published and meetings held are not results.

## Agency, freelancer, fractional, or in-house  (FW-OPS-001, with FW-OPS-004)

**When this matters:**
- You are deciding who should do a piece of marketing work.
- You are about to make your first marketing hire, or considering bringing a function in-house.
- A vendor has run a core function for 18 months or more and nobody inside understands it.
- You need to compare the cost of a hire with an agency retainer.

**Skip it when:**
- You are pre-product-market fit and the growth motion is still unproven. Keep outside spend small and scoped, and test before you commit to a structure.
- Total outside spend is under $15,000 a year and run by one person with no quality issues.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: Outside help is chosen one job at a time, with no view on what should stay external.
- Tier 2 Building: First explicit make-versus-buy calls for core functions, usually at Series A.
- Tier 3 Operating: Every major capability is marked always in-house, transitioning (with a trigger), or permanently outsourced.
- Tier 4 Compounding: The policy is reviewed each year, you keep a standing view of vendor alternatives and rates, and in-housing plans exist before the trigger hits.

**First build, in order**
1. For each capability (demand generation, content, paid media, SEO, events, design, web, PR, video), pick one label: **A** always in-house, **B** transitioning, or **C** permanently outsourced because volume or specialty does not justify a hire. Do this before choosing any vendor. A function labeled B needs a different contract from a C: add knowledge transfer and transition help.
2. For every B, write a measurable trigger (an ARR level, a headcount, a budget share, or a named hire), a ramp estimate, an overlap period with the vendor, and the documents the vendor must hand over.
3. Match the provider type to the work:
   - **Agency:** a team and process, for work that needs coordination and enough volume to justify the overhead.
   - **Freelancer:** one skilled person on clearly scoped, repeatable work (copy, design, content, ad creative). You need enough skill to brief and review it.
   - **Fractional specialist:** a senior person who directs and builds alongside you, such as a fractional CMO or demand-gen lead. Fits before the first hire.
   - **Consultant:** a fixed project with an end date (an audit, a positioning review, a stack review).
   - **Platform:** see the tool section above.
4. Review the policy every year at planning time.

**Decision rules**
- If you are a seed company asking "agency or freelancer?", then pick a freelancer for scoped, repeatable work. Pick an agency only when the work needs a coordinated team (paid media strategy plus execution, or SEO with technical, content, and link work). Most seed companies buy agency overhead before they need it.
- If you ask "fractional CMO or agency?", then hire the fractional when you need someone to decide what to build. Hire the agency when you already know what to build and need hands. Agencies execute against a brief, they do not supply strategy.
- If you hire a fractional, then write the mandate as builder of systems, not executor of campaigns. Ask each quarter: what system exists now that did not exist at the start of the quarter? Otherwise you pay senior rates for junior output.
- If you want to know how many vendors to run, then at seed hold zero to two. At Series A with a small team hold two to four, in your highest-value channels. A rush to hire many agencies early means channel priorities are not clear yet.
- If you are at Series A and weighing a demand-gen hire against an agency, then the agency is usually cost-competitive: scope is uncertain and a new hire needs ramp time. At Series B, in-house wins if the work is steady enough to fill a role.
- If you are choosing the first function to bring in-house, then content is usually the earliest. An in-house content manager knows the product and customers better, at similar or lower cost.
- If you are at Series B and a full-service agency covers five of your functions, then the team has not grown into its job. Use specialists, and plan to in-house one or two functions (content, SEO, demand generation, and design are the usual ones).
- If an agency's cost per approved deliverable runs above 1.5x a freelancer's rate, then ask what the extra coordination buys. Above 2x, the overhead is not justified.
- If you cannot fill a role at market rate, then try in order: a senior fractional at 10 to 20 hours a week (often better than a junior full-timer at the same cost), a contractor for a six-month build, or an agency where the minimum skill level is high (paid media at scale, enterprise PR).
- If the same role has been filled and left more than once in 18 months and the skill is hard to find or afford, then reconsider it as an agency or fractional job.
- If you are about to hire your first marketer and already have two or more agencies, then before the start date: score the portfolio, decide which vendors the hire inherits, restructures, or ends, and write down why. Otherwise the hire spends 30 to 60 days rebuilding that context.
- If your deals are over $50,000 a year, then a specialist agency that understands buying committees is worth a real premium. If deals are under $5,000, freelancers or productized services fit better.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| In-house demand-gen manager vs agency (Series A) | In-house total compensation $120,000-180,000 a year. Agency retainer $96,000-180,000 a year | Lintel guidance; check current local pay before deciding |
| In-house content manager vs agency or freelance pool | In-house $90,000-140,000. Outside $60,000-120,000 | Lintel guidance; same caution |
| Outside spend as a share of marketing budget | Falls from about 40-55% at Series A to about 20-40% at Series B and 15-25% at Series C+. Above range for two quarters, look for functions to in-house | Lintel guidance; the source gives slightly different ranges in two places, so treat as direction |
| Fractional CMO | $10,000-25,000 a month for 15-20 hours a week. $4,000-10,000 a month for advisory (4-8 hours a week) | Lintel guidance; check current rates |
| Time spent on vendor management | Series A small team: 10-20% of one person's time. Series B: 20-30% of a marketing ops manager's role | Lintel guidance |
| Dedicated vendor or partner ops role | Only when outside spend is above $500,000 a year and there are six or more strategic vendors | Lintel guidance |
| Fractional marketing ops covering vendors | Fits when outside spend is $200,000-500,000 a year | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:**
- Capability lock-in: a core function sits with an agency for 18 months or more, and you cannot hire for it, manage it, or improve it because nobody inside understands it.
- Over-agency early on: you pay a retainer over $5,000/month and cannot produce a written brief for any current assignment.
- Strategy tax: you hire an execution vendor and expect it to bring strategy.
- Fractional misuse: a senior builder ends up writing copy and running campaigns.

## Hiring marketers: role, process, onboarding  (FW-OPS-004)

**When this matters:**
- You are about to make a first dedicated hire and have not written what the role must produce in 90 days.
- Headcount grew faster than output.
- Turnover passed 25% in 12 months and the exits were not mainly about performance.
- You hired the same function twice in 18 months without finding out why the first hire failed.
- New hires learn the job by wandering around.

**Skip it when:**
- No hire is planned for six months and the team's skills fit current programs.
- You are pre-product-market fit. Hiring criteria will change once the motion is proven. Install the basics (role definition, a work sample, a 30/60/90 plan) and stop.
- The team is one person. A formal talent system costs more than it returns.
- A strategy change or reorganization is underway. Role definitions written now will be out of date on arrival.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: "We need a marketing person" is the brief. Interviews vary by manager, no skills test, onboarding by immersion, feedback only when something breaks.
- Tier 2 Building: Written role definitions, a skills assessment in every search, a 30/60/90 plan ready before day one, biweekly 1:1s, annual reviews tied to goals, a basic career ladder, and tracked turnover.
- Tier 3 Operating: Hires follow a team plan tied to strategy, onboarding is consistent, reviews run quarterly, an annual talent calibration happens, and retention risk is assessed.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Hires happen ahead of the gap, the team plan is updated every quarter, promotion from inside is common, and turnover sits at or below benchmark.

**First build, in order**
1. **Write the role definition before the job post (about one hour for the first draft).** Six parts: purpose in one sentence of business outcome, three to five outputs for the first 90 days, required skills, context that makes the role harder than it looks (budget, team size, stage, market), what success looks like at six months, and failure signals visible within 60 days. The job post, interview scorecard, onboarding plan, and 90-day goals all come from this page.
2. **Look ahead.** Hiring takes about 30 days and ramp takes about 90, so the person is fully useful roughly four months out. Define the role against the constraint you expect in 90 to 120 days, not today's pain.
3. **Build the skills assignment before you see any candidate (3 to 4 hours, then reuse).** Examples: content, a 400-word intro on an industry topic; demand gen, pick three accounts to prioritize from sample data and say why; marketing ops, sketch a lead scoring model from given inputs. Cap candidate time at 2 to 4 hours. Write a rubric of three to five dimensions (good, adequate, poor) first.
4. **Set the interview path with a pass or fail standard at each stage:** resume screen for context fit (prior stage, GTM motion, function); 30-minute phone screen (role understanding, motivation, pay alignment); the take-home; a 60 to 90 minute panel in a structured behavioral format (STAR) with at least three interviewers who each own one dimension; three references, at least two of them direct managers.
5. **Score each finalist** on five dimensions, 1 to 4 each, total 5 to 20: skills match, context fit (similar stage, motion, market), strategic judgment, builder versus manager orientation (matches what the role needs), and reliability and follow-through. Read the total: 17 to 20 strong hire, make the offer and document why. 13 to 16 good with fixable gaps, offer with a 90-day plan that names the gap and tell the candidate. 9 to 12 marginal, hire only if the pool is exhausted and the role cannot wait, with 60-day milestones. 5 to 8 do not hire.
6. **Ask references four pointed questions** instead of "would you recommend them": a program this person built from scratch and its result; one piece of feedback you gave them that made them better; where they thrive and where they struggle; and whether you would hire them again for a team you were building, and why. A person who truly backs them answers the last one fast and with specifics.
7. **Send a 30/60/90 plan at offer acceptance.** Days 1 to 14: read the ICP, messaging, competitive and strategy documents, meet the team, sales, customer success, and two or three customers. Days 15 to 30: first deliverable and first program ownership. Days 31 to 60: owns primary programs, starts reporting, first performance talk. Days 61 to 90: full ramp, gap analysis done, development plan drafted. Hold a formal 30-minute check at day 30. Include compliance basics (FW-OPS-002).

**Decision rules**
- If your first hire comes before product-market fit, then ask whether a growth motion has been validated. If not, a channel specialist is optimizing something unproven. The right hire is a growth-minded generalist who runs structured experiments. Content or brand marketers before the motion works are the most common wrong seed hire.
- If a growth driver is validated, then hire a specialist in that driver. Generalists spread thin across eight activities and give slightly better marketing everywhere but no acceleration.
- If you are not a marketer and the base pay is above $80,000, then get a marketing expert (an advisor, a board member, a fractional CMO) to review the work sample and sit in on at least one interview before the offer.
- If the same role failed twice in 18 months, then diagnose before posting again. Likely causes, in order: the role was defined too loosely, onboarding set unclear expectations, or the role needs skills you cannot find or afford (then consider an agency or fractional).
- If your motion is sales-led, then weigh pipeline-building experience at a similar deal size and sales cycle, and ability to work with sales. If it is product-led, weigh product analytics, testing, and lifecycle work, and be wary of a strong content or brand marketer who cannot connect to activation. If it is partner-led, test directly for channel marketing, since it looks like demand gen on a resume but is a different skill.
- If a hire is off plan at day 60, then split the cause. A context gap gets missing information plus a 30-day recovery plan. A skills gap gets a direct talk naming the gap, a 30-day recheck, and an exit if there is no change. Most context problems recover and most skills problems do not.
- If you feel pressed to hire fast, then do not shorten the evaluation. A marginal hire made out of desperation leaves the program further behind at day 90.
- If one candidate is a dazzling interviewer, then rely on the work sample and references. Interview skill and job skill are different.
- If you hear "great culture fit," then check whether it means comfort. Candidates who challenge your assumptions are harder to read and can be the better hire.
- If you plan to promote your best individual contributor to manager, then check for management readiness first (see the developing section below).

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Time to full productivity | 60-90 days for an individual contributor with structured onboarding. 90-120 for a leader. Above 120 days means onboarding failure or a skills mismatch | Lintel guidance |
| Hires meeting expectations at 12 months | 75% or more is healthy. Below 60% means the process is failing. Above 90% may mean you are too cautious | Lintel guidance |
| Average finalist rubric score | Above 14 of 20 above coordinator level. Below 12 means weak sourcing or a lowered bar | Lintel guidance |
| Skills assessment used | 90% or more of hires above coordinator level | Lintel guidance |
| New hire onboarding rating at day 30 (1-10) | 7 or higher. Below 6 means structural gaps | Lintel guidance |
| Time to fill | Above 60 days, audit the process for bottlenecks | Lintel guidance |
| Search fees | Contingency 15-20% of first-year base. Retained 25-30% | Lintel guidance |
| Cost of a failed manager-level hire | Roughly $80,000-150,000 in direct and indirect cost | Lintel estimate, rough |

**Where it goes wrong:**
- The role is defined by a stock job description, so you hire an excellent person for the wrong job.
- Only interviews, no skills test, so the offer goes to the best talker.
- Onboarding by immersion: a laptop, a calendar of intro meetings, and "absorb the business."
- You hire for yesterday's problem. The pipeline gap that prompted the hire has turned into a retention problem by the time they ramp.
- A hiring manager who is a marketer picks people like themselves. Anchor on the role definition instead.

## What to hire first at each stage  (FW-OPS-004, with FW-OPS-003 and FW-OPS-001)

**When this matters:**
- You are deciding the next marketing hire, or whether to hire at all.
- You are asked to justify headcount to a founder or finance.
- Marketing headcount is growing without a plan for what comes next.

**Skip it when:**
- The growth motion is not defined. If you cannot state your marketing strategy in one sentence, a hire is premature (settle FW-FOUND-005 and FW-FOUND-009 first).
- A strategy pivot or reorganization is in progress.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: You hire into the most visible pain.
- Tier 2 Building: A written team plan sequences hires against priorities.
- Tier 3 Operating: The team plan is built from the marketing strategy and shows current state, state at 12 months, and the gap.
- Tier 4 Compounding: The plan is reviewed quarterly and drives hiring order. Hires come ahead of the gap.

**First build, in order**
1. **Pre-product-market fit (seed):** no dedicated marketing hire. The founder owns marketing, the stack stays free-tier, and outside help is zero to two freelancers on scoped work. If you need a senior voice, use a quarterly marketing advisor.
2. **Seed, to find the motion, or motion just validated:** one growth-minded generalist who runs structured experiments. Once a channel is proven, hire depth in that channel.
3. **Series A (about 15 to 75 employees), first or second dedicated hire:** a builder. Someone who has started a function from nothing, is comfortable with thin resources, thinks and executes, and has done the channel your validated motion needs. Avoid a big-company marketer who needs infrastructure to function. If you need strategic leadership and cannot fund a full-time senior hire, use a fractional CMO at 15 to 20 hours a week.
4. **Series A, next hires:** specialists in your top channel. Content is often the first function to bring in-house. Keep agencies for work where the minimum skill level is high (paid media at scale, enterprise PR). When stack cost reaches $3,000 to $8,000/month and governance is needed but volume is thin, a fractional marketing ops person fits.
5. **Series B (about 75 to 250 employees):** specialization. Hire a full-time marketing ops manager when the stack costs over $8,000/month, has over 15 tools, integration gaps keep corrupting data, and the marketing lead spends over 5 hours a week on administration. Add a first management layer (a lead managing two to three people) when the team grows from about 3 to 8 people. Hire a full-time VP or CMO when you have a validated growth motion, a team of 5 or more, a marketing budget of $1 million or more, and strategic decisions arriving daily.
6. **Series C+ (250+ employees):** a dedicated vendor or partner operations role when outside spend passes $500,000 a year with six or more strategic vendors. Watch team balance: strategists without operational depth, or executors who cannot think strategically.
7. **Whatever the stage, hand the new person a documented starting point.** A marketing leader should inherit a documented ICP, GTM motion, basic metrics, a small team with role definitions, the current stack, and the sales relationship. The leader builds the system. The leader should not be hired to invent strategy from nothing, which is the founder's job. A marketing ops hire should inherit the tool inventory, renewal calendar, and purchase gate, then build in order: a health score and stack map in 30 days, a rubric and utilization tracking in 60, a tool roadmap, a TCO model for the top five tools, and a renewal plan in 90.

**Decision rules**
- If you ask "full-time CMO or fractional?", then pick full-time when you are Series B or later, expect a team of 10 or more within 12 months, make strategic calls daily, and can pay market rate. Pick fractional when you are Series A or earlier, need guidance more than management, lack the budget, or need cover while a search runs. A fractional is a different model, not a cheaper version.
- If a leader starts, then expect by day 30 a team assessment and a 12-month strategy frame, by day 60 a hiring plan, a vendor roster review, and a measurement frame, and by day 90 a full strategy with budget, a team plan, and a Q1 program plan. Expect a first-time leader to take 90 to 120 days to reach full speed.
- If a founder with no marketing background hires a leader, then weight work samples and context fit most, and call direct managers.
- If marketing headcount grew over 50% in a year, then fix management span and onboarding before hiring more.
- If a candidate is strong on executing but you need a leader (or the reverse), then do not hire. Hiring a strategist when you need a builder, or a builder when you need a strategist, is the most common leadership mismatch.
- If a leader candidate cannot describe how they measured their own pipeline contribution, has never built from scratch, or claims team results as their own, then pass.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Dedicated marketing headcount as share of company | Seed 0-3%. Series A 5-10% (2-4 people at 40 employees). Series B 8-12% (10-15 at 120). Series C+ 8-15% | Lintel guidance, rough |
| Marketing ops hire triggers | Stack over $8,000/month, 15+ tools, over 5 hours a week of admin | Lintel guidance |
| Full-time CMO triggers | Series B or later, team of 5+, marketing budget of $1M+, daily strategic calls | Lintel guidance |
| First management layer | Team of about 8, one lead managing 2-3 people | Lintel guidance |
| Ramp after a hire | About 4 months from decision (30 to hire, 90 to ramp) | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:**
- You hire a generalist who spreads across eight activities and produces no acceleration, when one strong channel hire was what the company needed.
- You hire a specialist for an unproven motion (a demand-gen specialist for a product-led motion that has not been validated).
- You promote your best individual contributor to manager without assessing management skill.
- You hire before the motion is defined, so the person is qualified but pointed at the wrong job.

## Developing and keeping marketers  (FW-OPS-004)

**When this matters:**
- Skills on the team look no better than a year ago.
- A strong performer just resigned or shows warning signs.
- Several people have left the same manager.
- Someone is underperforming and nobody has told them.
- The team is growing fast and 1:1s are slipping.

**Skip it when:**
- The team is one person.
- You are pre-product-market fit, or a strategy change or reorganization is in progress.
- The team is performing well and turnover risk is genuinely low. Run the basics and revisit when growth adds risk.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: No written expectations, feedback only when something goes wrong, no development plans, retention handled by reacting to resignations.
- Tier 2 Building: Role definitions, biweekly 1:1s, annual development conversations, promotion criteria written down, exit interviews collected.
- Tier 3 Operating: Quarterly performance reviews, yearly talent calibration, succession plans for the two most critical roles, twice-yearly retention risk checks.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Development spending is tied to skills the roadmap needs, promotion from inside is common, and turnover is at or below benchmark.

**First build, in order**
1. **Write a one-page role definition for each person (2 to 3 hours in total).** Three outputs expected in the next 90 days, what good looks like, and what would show they are off track. Share it. An unshared expectation is not an expectation.
2. **Start biweekly 30-minute 1:1s with a fixed agenda:** what you did against the role page, what is next, one thing I can do to help, and one piece of feedback from me. The feedback item is mandatory, even when everything is good.
3. **Add a quarterly 60-minute review** (last quarter against the role, next quarter's priorities, one development area) and an annual review that summarizes a year of feedback. If the annual review is your main feedback channel, gaps sit for months.
4. **Define levels:** Coordinator or Associate, Manager, Senior Manager, Director, VP or CMO. For each level write the scope, skills, decision freedom, and observable behaviors. Write promotion criteria in behaviors ("owned a program with more than three cross-functional stakeholders without escalation"), require 90 days of performing at the next level, and calibrate against recent promotions.
5. **Run a yearly development plan for each person:** two or three skills the company will need from them in the next 12 months, their current level, and a specific action for each gap. Stretch assignments beat courses ("own the next attribution model build, reviewed by a senior colleague"). Review quarterly.
6. **Check retention twice a year.** For each person rate engagement, flight risk, and impact of their leaving. High flight risk plus high impact is a priority: act before they get an offer. Hold a structured exit interview for every voluntary departure and review the pattern twice a year.
7. **At Tier 3, run a yearly talent calibration (a 3x3 grid of performance against potential, popularized by McKinsey for GE).** The usual failure is everyone landing in the middle box. If that happens, the exercise gave you no information.

**Decision rules**
- If someone has performed below expectations for over 30 days and nobody has said so, then hold the direct conversation within 5 business days. Name the specific situation, the behavior, and its impact (the SBI format). If nothing improves in 30 days, start a formal improvement plan. Do not extend a plan at the midpoint without new information.
- If you are unsure whether someone is underperforming, then your expectations were not written down. Write the role page first, then judge output against it.
- If a high performer shows warning signs (quieter in 1:1s, asking about career path), then act before an offer arrives. In order: give them a program they want to own, adjust title if they are working above level, review pay if below band, and have an honest conversation about where they want to be in 18 months.
- If a counter-offer would put someone above their manager or band, then pause. Reactive pay moves spread through the team. Do not hand out senior titles without senior scope either.
- If several people left the same manager, then it is a management problem. Run exit interviews, set specific behavior targets, and expect change within 90 days.
- If you are considering promoting an individual contributor to manager, then check four signs: they help others without being asked, give peers direct feedback, think about programs and process, and handle ambiguity without escalating. If fewer than three are clearly true, offer expanded individual scope first.
- If the talent health score is low across several areas, then fix in this order: unmanaged underperformers, retention of high performers, skill gaps limiting programs, then development pace and hiring quality.
- If marketing grew from about 3 to 8 people in a year, then add the first management layer now, before 1:1 quality drops and people go unmanaged.

**Team talent health scorecard.** Five dimensions scored 1 to 4, total 5 to 20, run quarterly: capability coverage, performance distribution (4 means 25% or more clear high performers, 60% or more solid core, under 15% underperformers), development velocity, retention health, and hiring process quality (4 means 80% or more of recent hires meeting expectations at 12 months). Read the total: 17 to 20 healthy. 13 to 16 sound, fix the lowest within 60 days. 9 to 12 meaningful risk. 5 to 8 serious dysfunction.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Voluntary attrition | 12-18% a year is typical. Below 8% may mean underperformers are not being moved on. Above 25% is a retention or culture problem | Lintel guidance |
| High-performer attrition | Should run below overall attrition. Above 10% is a warning. Above 15% is critical, assess remaining high performers within 30 days | Lintel guidance |
| Internal promotion rate | 30-50% healthy. Below 20% means weak development. Above 70% may mean too little outside perspective | Lintel guidance |
| Structured feedback conversations | At least 6 per person per quarter. Below 4 means the cadence is not holding | Lintel guidance |
| Development plan coverage | 100% above coordinator level. Below 70% means it is treated as optional | Lintel guidance |
| Learning budget | About 1-3% of marketing payroll | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:**
- Feedback avoidance: a visible gap sits for 60 to 90 days because the manager dreads the talk, then gets addressed at month six.
- Development by default: capability a year later looks like capability at hire.
- Title inflation as a retention tool, with no change in scope, which resets expectations and pay equity within a year.
- Reactive pay bands: counter-offers that put someone above their manager and teach the team to interview elsewhere.

## Dropped

Left out or cut because they could not be trusted, conflicted, or were unsupported:
- **Source credits on benchmarks.** The library credits the tool-spend share (15-25%) and headcount share ranges to named research-firm surveys and investor benchmark reports, and its own note says no reliable survey splits tool spend by stage. I kept the ranges as rough Lintel guidance and removed all names. Treat them as sanity checks.
- **Agency waste claim.** "20-35% of agency retainer spend is wasted without a management system" has no source. Dropped.
- **Consolidation savings percentages.** The library gives three different figures for what an audit saves (15-25%, 15-30%, 20-35%), plus a first-year $15,000-60,000 savings figure and a "40-60% of renegotiations succeed" figure. They disagree or have no source. Dropped. Dollar examples from the "what good looks like" sections are illustrations, not benchmarks, and were not used.
- **Vendor count in the market** (10,000+ tools). Not needed and unverified. Dropped.
- **Outside spend as a share of budget.** Two sections of FW-OPS-001 give different ranges for each stage. I kept the direction and rounded the Series A, B, and C+ ranges. I dropped the seed percentage and all absolute dollar ranges for outside spend by stage (they sit oddly against seed-stage stack costs).
- **Cost per approved deliverable in dollars** ($400-800 long-form, $150-350 ads, $80-200 social). Too dependent on market and year. Kept only the ratio rule against freelance rates.
- **Base salary bands by level** (coordinator to CMO), cost per hire, and training or conference spend figures. They go stale fast and carry no source. Kept only the fractional CMO fee ranges, search fee percentages, and the failed-hire cost estimate, each marked rough and to be checked.
- **Talent health score "healthy" line.** The library says 15+ is healthy in the metrics section and 17 to 20 in the scoring section. I used the scoring bands.
- **Utilization wording.** The library measures tool utilization two ways (percent usage with 50% and 30% cutoffs, and a 1 to 5 rating with 3 as the line). Both are kept and shown as written. They are different measures, so use the percent rule for cancel decisions on a single tool and the rating for the stack-level rate.
- **Spend thresholds that differ by step.** The library uses $200/month for the inventory and purchase gate, $500/month for the scoring rubric and renewal process, $1,000/month for TCO. I kept all three and labeled where each applies.
- **Compensation, hiring law, and PIP legal steps.** Not mine to answer. Get an HR professional or lawyer for improvement plans, exits, and contracts.
- **FW-OPS-002** (compliance and legal) is not covered here beyond pointers. It belongs in its own reference file.
