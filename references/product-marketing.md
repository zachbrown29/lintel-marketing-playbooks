# Product Marketing

How a seed to Series B B2B software company turns what the product does into a story reps can sell, then keeps that story current through buyer research, competitor prep, sales content, launches, and a feedback loop to the roadmap. For a founder, a first marketer, or a first product marketer.

**Owns:** setting up product marketing and in what order; deploying and governing positioning once written; win/loss and buyer research; battlecards and rep training; sales enablement content; launches and launch levels; communicating a pricing or packaging change; the feedback loop from deals to the roadmap; the readiness test for category creation.
**Does not own:** writing the core positioning statement and messaging architecture (FW-FOUND-003, FW-FOUND-004); the ICP and personas (FW-FOUND-001, FW-FOUND-002); the GTM motion (FW-FOUND-005); designing the competitor research program, sources, and cadence (FW-DATA-008); what to charge and how to package (FW-GROWTH-005); campaigns to win a rival's customers (FW-EMERGING-011); entering a new segment or market (FW-EMERGING-012); choosing a category path and analyst work (FW-EMERGING-005, FW-INFLUENCE-001); press (FW-PR-001); sales process and stages (FW-SALES-003).

A note on names. The source library calls launch sizes "Tier 1, 2, 3." This file says Minor, Significant, and Major launch so they never mix with the maturity ladder (Tier 1 Reactive to Tier 4 Compounding).

## Product marketing system: where to start and in what order  (FW-PM-001)

**When this matters:** Reps pitch the product differently on every call and close rates vary by rep. A launch or pricing change is coming with no plan. Lost deals say "they did not see what makes you different." The founder or an engineer gets pulled into demos. A rival just challenged your main claim.
**Skip it when:** You have fewer than 5 paying customers and product-market fit is unconfirmed (talk to customers; this function amplifies a validated story and cannot invent one). The founders carry the story on every call. The ICP, personas, and messaging architecture are undefined. Pipeline volume is the real constraint and a minimum positioning exists (product marketing lifts close rate and does not fill an empty funnel).

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: Reps pitch their own version. A deck and a website describe features. No battlecards or launch process.
- Tier 2 Building: One written positioning document, one standard deck, win/loss on recent deals, battlecards for the top rivals.
- Tier 3 Operating: Positioning by segment, a launch playbook, battlecards, and sales content across the buying journey, with a named owner and a charter.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Win/loss, rival tracking, and launch data feed a quarterly positioning update and the roadmap.

**First build, in order** (3 to 5 hours a week, about 4 weeks)
1. Interview 5 to 8 customers who bought fast, renewed without a fight, and use the product heavily. Ask: what problem were you solving, what alternatives did you consider, how do you measure whether it works. Skip features. Take verbatim notes. About 3 to 5 hours of calls plus 2 to synthesize.
2. Write a 1 to 2 page positioning statement from their words (method in FW-FOUND-003). Have the CEO and two reps challenge it. Ask reps "does this match what you hear in deals," never "do you like it."
3. Build one standard deck from it: 12 slides at most, with technical detail, comparisons, and pricing in an appendix. Done when two reps use it as their baseline.
4. Write the charter: what product marketing produces, what it only contributes to, and what is out of scope. Get marketing, sales, and product heads to agree. Without it the role becomes a content shop within about 90 days.
5. Then build: messaging document, win/loss program, battlecards (all three start from positioning), launch levels, sales content, a rep onboarding module, a quarterly positioning review, and the roadmap input.

**Decision rules**
- If you are seed stage and founder-led, then write a two-page positioning document from buyer interviews plus one deck, and stop. No PMM hire, battlecard library, or launch playbook until the first sales hire cannot carry the story without the founder.
- If you are Series A with 4 or more reps and close rates differ a lot by rep, then do positioning, a deck from it, win/loss on a sample of the last 10 closed deals, and battlecards for the top 3 rivals. Adding reps multiplies this problem.
- If you must choose between a PMM and another rep, then pick the PMM when close-rate variance is high, competitive win rate trails overall, or ramp is long. Pick the rep when pipeline is short and close rate is healthy.
- If one person does product marketing on top of other work, then do positioning upkeep and the core content set (deck, one-pager, objection guide) first, then win/loss and battlecards. Defer the category narrative, a formal launch playbook, and roadmap input.
- If you are PLG, then website and in-product messaging that matches positioning comes before sales content.
- If you are weighing a first PMM hire, then check you have a confirmed ICP, 2 or more personas, 4 or more reps, 20 or more closed deals, and a clear problem for the hire. Write the charter before the job post. Hire full-time past about 5 reps. Use a fractional person for 3 to 6 months of build work, never for rival monitoring (a monthly check-in misses the move that lands in week 3).
- If a rival announces something that hits your main difference, then hold a positioning review within 5 business days: check credibility with product, find the next defensible difference, update that card, brief sales first.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Win rate on sales-qualified deals | SMB inbound 25 to 40%, mid-market 15 to 30%, enterprise 10 to 25% | Lintel guidance. Below range, run win/loss |
| Top-quartile rep close rate vs median | Under 1.5x is healthy; over 2x means the best reps know something the system lacks | Lintel guidance |
| Reps delivering the core claim without drifting | 70% or more at Tier 2; under 50% means materials go unused | Lintel guidance |
| Win rate drop over a quarter | More than 5 points: 5 buyer interviews within 2 weeks | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** Positioning by committee comes out generic ("industry-leading," "end-to-end"), so one person drafts and others challenge. The deck is frozen after the fundraise. With no charter the PMM becomes a deck factory. The PMM never hears live calls, so materials drift from how buyers talk (block about 4 hours a month to listen).

## Positioning and messaging in use: deploy, govern, test  (FW-PM-002)

**When this matters:** The website, deck, and outbound emails read like three companies. Close rates follow pitch quality, not territory. A new segment, major launch, or first sales cohort is coming. Losses say "did not understand what you do."
**Skip it when:** You have fewer than 10 paying customers and are still in discovery (it gets rebuilt). The ICP is unconfirmed. A validated document under 12 months old exists and the real gap is training. Building the base statement is FW-FOUND-003 and FW-FOUND-004; this block starts once one exists.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: A tagline and a deck. No document saying why the claims are true.
- Tier 2 Building: A positioning document and a messaging document: main claim, proof points, persona variants, top 8 objection answers.
- Tier 3 Operating: Variants per main segment, a named owner, a quarterly review, and a list of every channel to update when positioning changes.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Updates run on a fixed cycle driven by win/loss, rival tracking, and buyer research.

**First build, in order**
1. Interview the happiest customers (above) and add: "what would you do if this disappeared tomorrow." That puts spreadsheets, doing nothing, and hiring someone on the alternatives list, not only named software.
2. Write the five-part statement (competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, target customers, market category; model from April Dunford), 300 to 500 words. One person writes, others challenge, the writer decides.
3. Change the deck headline and homepage headline to match. Until they change, nothing outside the building has moved.
4. Pull one main value claim and 3 to 5 proof points. Record each point's evidence, source, date, and persona. Flag evidence over 18 months old.
5. Write variants for the champion, economic buyer, and technical evaluator: same claim, different concern. The economic buyer variant is the first gap in sales-led motions. Interview 3 who approved a purchase about the outcome they owned, the risk they managed, and what would have made them say no.
6. Build the objection guide from the prospect's own words. Answer every objection in 10% or more of deals: acknowledge, reframe to your main claim, give evidence. Test each in 3 live deals.
7. Write a one-page channel guide. Problem-level messages for ads, cold email, and posts. Capability-level for website and nurture. Proof-level for decks, comparison pages, and late-stage leave-behinds.
8. Set governance: a named owner, a 90-minute quarterly review, a propagation list, and a change log with date, reason, and approver.

**Decision rules**
- If losses say "did not understand what we do," then test the category name with 5 buyers and rewrite the claim problem first, capability second. Deploy in 10 deals and see if that reason fades.
- If a rival now matches your main claim, then find the next defensible attribute. If none exists, ask buyers "besides that, what else mattered" and use the answer.
- If a proof point is challenged in 3 deals in a row, then strengthen the evidence within 30 days or drop the claim until you can back it.
- If a new objection appears in 15% or more of deals, then add a response within 2 weeks and test it before finalizing.
- If you want to test messaging, then do not ask buyers if they like it (they say yes). Use it in the first five minutes of live calls or A/B test headlines against conversion.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Days since last positioning review | Under 120; over 180 in a competitive market is a governance failure | Lintel guidance |
| Content matching the document (quarterly audit) | 85% or more at Tier 3; under 70% means governance is failing | Lintel guidance |
| Proof point challenge rate | Under 10% healthy; over 25% means thin or overstated evidence | Lintel guidance |
| Claims a rival can match equally | Target zero; over 30% means update now | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** The statement lists features, not buyer outcomes. A workshop produces consensus language. The team updates the deck and not the positioning, so channels diverge again. Proof points borrowed from one big customer collapse when a buyer asks for evidence.

## Win/loss and buyer research  (FW-PM-006, with research steps in FW-PM-001 and FW-PM-002)

**When this matters:** You have lost deals for the same reasons for two quarters and never asked the buyers. Competitive win rate is falling and sales and product disagree why. You are about to change positioning, pricing, the roadmap, or the ICP with no buyer input. Your only data is the loss code a rep picked in the CRM.
**Skip it when:** You have fewer than 10 closed deals (each interview is an anecdote). You are pre-PMF. A full program ran in the last 90 days and nothing big changed (apply findings first).

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: Rep notes and CRM loss codes. Nobody outside the deal asked the buyer.
- Tier 2 Building: A written guide, an interviewer who did not work the deal, a quarterly rhythm, and a set route from findings to positioning.
- Tier 3 Operating: 10 to 15 interviews a quarter, confidence-rated findings, direct links to cards, positioning, and the roadmap.
- Tier 4 Compounding: A multi-quarter record shows drift in buyer criteria and rival strength before deal data does.

**First build, in order**
1. Pick 5 deals from the last 60 days that reached real evaluation. Default mix: 3 wins, 2 losses. If close rate is the problem, flip to 3 losses, 2 wins.
2. Send a three-sentence request framed as research on how companies evaluate tools like yours. Do not say win, loss, or the deal. A founder, PMM, or other non-commercial person runs the call, never the rep. After a loss, wait 2 to 3 weeks so it does not read as recovery.
3. Ask three questions in order: what problem were you solving, what did you evaluate and what mattered most, what was the most important factor in the final decision. Verbatim notes. Let them finish, wait about 3 seconds, and repeat their words back to dig deeper.
4. Write one page: what surprised you, how buyers describe the problem (versus your words), which alternatives they weighed (versus your assumed set). One finding that contradicts an internal belief proves the program is worth building. 6 to 8 hours total.
5. For the full program: deals above an ACV floor that reached a demo or evaluation and ended won or lost; about half wins, never under 40%; 10 to 15 interviews a quarter (8 is the minimum for patterns); 20 to 30 minute calls.
6. Analyze in layers. A finding needs 3 or more interviews (a single comment is an observation). Rate confidence: High (unprompted by 3 or more), Medium (2, or inferred), Low (one). Sort into positioning, competitive, process, and product, each with an owner and date.
7. Set the action route before the first analysis. Positioning: PMM told in 48 hours, review within 30 days if High. Competitive: card update within 5 business days. Process: sales lead told in 48 hours, on the next weekly call. Product: input document to product within 2 weeks of the quarterly cycle.
8. Send four short outputs, not one long report: a one-page executive summary (300 words: top 3 findings, one thing to protect, one urgent action), a two-page sales briefing, a product input, and the full PMM analysis kept internal.

**Decision rules**
- If the rep who worked the deal runs the interview, then change that first. It improves candor more than anything else.
- If you are seed stage, then the founder calls every prospect who declined and asks the three questions. Five conversations before any positioning or product investment.
- If you are Series A, then plan 5 to 8 interviews a quarter; at Series B, 10 to 12. Use an outside interviewer for losses if loss response is under 25%.
- If you are PLG or self-serve, then email users who cancelled or did not convert in the last 30 days: what were you hoping to do, what stopped you, what would have changed it.
- If deals are under about $5K ACV, then use a 3 to 5 question exit survey, 20 to 30 responses a quarter. In mid-market ($5K to $50K), interview the champion and economic buyer separately because they tell different stories.
- If losses are mostly "price," then do not cut price first. Interview 5 buyers on what they compared it to, check the ROI calculator is credible, and check if the economic buyer ever heard the value story. Only if buyers confirm you cost more than the value justifies, take it to FW-GROWTH-005.
- If CRM loss codes and buyer interviews disagree, then the gap is a finding. Frame it as CRM setup and coaching, not blame.
- If a major call is coming (pricing, ICP expansion, pivot, new market) with no data for that segment, then interview 5 to 8 buyers in 3 to 4 weeks first.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Interview response rate | Wins 60 to 75%; losses 25 to 45%; under 20% for losses means timing or sender is off | Lintel guidance. Try 3 weeks later and a neutral sender |
| High-confidence findings that challenge a held belief | 30 to 40%; under 20% suggests confirmation bias or leading questions | Lintel guidance |
| High-confidence findings with an action in 60 days | 100%; under 70% means the route is broken | Lintel guidance |
| Loss outreach | One follow-up after 7 days, two contacts total; a $25 to $50 gift card for losses only | Lintel guidance |
| Budget | Seed $0 (4 to 6 founder hours a quarter); Series A $500 to $2,000 a quarter; Series B $3,000 to $10,000 | Lintel guidance. Outsource loss interviews first |

**Where it goes wrong:** Reps run their own interviews and hear the safe answers ("price," "timing"). A 15-page report nobody reads. The program is used to win an internal argument, so questions test hypotheses instead of listening (test them only in the last 5 minutes, labeled). It runs only after bad quarters, so there is no trend record. The interviewer defends the company and the buyer goes quiet. Only the champion is interviewed.

## Competitive battlecards and rep training  (FW-PM-004)

**When this matters:** Competitive win rate sits clearly below overall win rate. Reps answer "how do you compare to X" differently. A rival launched, repriced, or shifted positioning and sales has no plan. You keep losing to the same one or two names at the final decision.
**Skip it when:** There are no real direct competitors (the rival is "do nothing" or "build it ourselves," which needs the status quo card below). Positioning is unstable. You are pre-PMF and losses come from missing product. Research program design is FW-DATA-008; winning a rival's customers is FW-EMERGING-011.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: Rival answers live in the founder's head. Reps improvise.
- Tier 2 Building: Cards for each rival in 10% or more of deals, one 30-minute training, basic monitoring of rival sites, review pages, and pricing pages.
- Tier 3 Operating: Cards updated after major rival moves, a quarterly report, "competitors present" tracked in the CRM, certification for new reps.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Win rate by rival and stage shows which card content works. Updates are proactive.

**First build, in order** (4 to 6 hours for the first card)
1. Ask 5 reps by call, not survey: "which rival do you meet most, and what do they say that is hardest to answer?" Their words become the card. Write a one-page profile of the top 3 first (method in FW-DATA-008).
2. Collect three inputs on the top rival: what the 2 reps with the best record say, 30 minutes on their site, pricing page, and recent reviews, and notes on lost deals.
3. Write a card of two pages at most (about 3 minutes to read), in two halves of equal weight. Proactive: 3 or 4 questions a rep asks early so the criteria you win on matter. Reactive: for the 3 to 5 most common scenarios, the prospect's words, a response that has worked in 2 to 4 spoken sentences, and proof. Add the top 3 reasons buyers pick you, the top 3 they pick the rival (be honest), and one "landmine" question that exposes the rival's weakness without naming them.
4. Train for 30 to 45 minutes with role-play on the 3 most common scenarios. A card with no training is a document.
5. Add a "competitors present" field to the CRM before the card goes live, for a baseline. Include "status quo," "build internally," and "other."
6. For the full program, map every alternative (direct rivals, adjacent products, do-it-yourself, status quo) by how often it appears, at what stage, and your win rate against it. Build cards by frequency times impact. Have a rep who did not write the card role-play a scenario to validate it.
7. Build the status quo card. Proactive: questions that put a number on the cost of the current way. Reactive: replies to "not ready," "other priorities," "we can build it," and "next quarter," each with a specific cost of delay.
8. Review each loss above an ACV floor: when the rival entered, what the buyer found compelling, which card elements the rep used, whether it was a product, price, positioning, or process gap, and what one change would have helped. Each review yields a card update, a positioning question, or a roadmap input.

**Decision rules**
- If competitive win rate trails overall and you are Tier 1, then write the card for the rival causing the most losses, train within 2 weeks, and track win rate against that rival for two quarters.
- If a rival makes a big announcement, then check credibility with product within 24 hours, update the card within 5 business days, and brief sales within a week.
- If you lose at the final decision, then the proactive half is the gap: the rival won on a criterion you never raised early. Ask 5 buyers what decided the final comparison.
- If a new rival shows up in 3 or more deals in a month, then a short watch card in 2 weeks and a full card within 30 days.
- If win rate against one rival has fallen for two quarters, then pull the last 5 losses and review each. Product gap: to product within 2 weeks. Positioning gap: rebuild the card within 3 weeks. Process gap: add a sales training topic.
- If under 50% of deals have the competitor field filled in, then do not act on competitive win rate yet. Above 75% is the floor to act; 80% is the Tier 3 standard.
- If you are bootstrapped or a one-person team, then use free alerts for rival names, a weekly 15-minute scan of their review pages, and cards for the top 3 rivals ranked by win-rate gap. Consider a platform only when one person spends over about 3 hours a week on manual tracking.
- If the quarterly report always says "all clear" while win rate is flat or falling, then add interviews with buyers who chose the rival, and rate each claim High, Medium, or Low confidence by how it was observed.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Card coverage | 100% of rivals in 10% or more of deals, each reviewed within 6 months | Lintel guidance |
| Competitive win rate vs overall | Within 5 to 10 points at Tier 3; over 15 points is a serious gap; a 5-point quarterly drop triggers review of the last 5 losses | Lintel guidance |
| Card use in competitive deals | 60% or more at Tier 2; under 30% is an access or training problem | Lintel guidance |
| Card freshness | 100% reviewed within 90 days; flag any over 180 days | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** The card is a feature table that gives a rep nothing to say. The card attacks the rival, which sounds defensive to a buyer who likes them (write in first person, from confidence). The card is never updated and reps repeat claims a buyer can refute. Inference is stated as fact (one hiring post becomes "they are entering enterprise"). Training is a 90-minute slide dump instead of role-play.

## Sales enablement content  (FW-PM-005)

**When this matters:** Reps build their own decks because the library lacks what they need. Deals stall at evaluation because the champion has nothing to show the person who signs. Losses say "could not get internal buy-in." New reps lose two months finding or building materials. A library exists but reps cannot find the right asset mid-call.
**Skip it when:** There are fewer than 3 reps and the founder is on most calls. Positioning is unstable. The ICP, personas, or sales stages are undefined (stages: FW-SALES-003).

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: Reps build their own materials. One deck at best.
- Tier 2 Building: A core set for the main ICP (deck, one-pager, objection guide, ROI framework, comparison), indexed by persona and stage.
- Tier 3 Operating: A library across all segments, personas, and stages, with usage tracked and gaps fed by deal data.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Content is judged by stage conversion and win rate, and refreshed from win/loss.

**First build, in order** (8 to 12 hours)
1. Gap audit: 15 minutes each with 3 reps. Ask "in your last deal, when did you need something you did not have" and "what do you build from scratch that you wish someone built." The 3 most common gaps are your first 3 assets.
2. Build or fix the deck from positioning. 10 slides: problem, why alternatives fall short, what you do differently, how it works, who uses it, proof, how to start, pricing overview if it fits, next steps, then an appendix. Core is slides 1 to 9, deliverable in 30 minutes plus 15 for questions.
3. One one-pager for the persona with the biggest gap. Champion: problem in their words, outcome, who uses it, cost of not solving, one call to action. Economic buyer: business impact, ROI summary, implementation risk, outcome proof. Technical evaluator: integrations, security, requirements, support. One page, strict.
4. Next, fix stalls at internal approval: an ROI calculator and a business case template the champion fills in. Build the calculator from interviews with 3 economic buyers who approved purchases. Buyer-entered inputs (users, hourly rate, current process time, expected gain), visible math, outputs of hours saved, dollars saved, and payback months. Keep it simple.
5. Case studies, each with at least one number. Open with the customer's problem in their words, then why they chose you, then the quantified outcome and a quote about the outcome and not the product. Make testimonial cards while full studies are in production.
6. A prospect-facing comparison per top rival, separate from the internal card. Cover the key buying dimensions, how each option performs on public facts only, and where each is the better choice. No attacks.
7. Email templates from the top 2 or 3 reps by reply rate. Subject under 50 characters, body under 150 words, one call to action.
8. Organize by stage, persona, and segment. Stages: 1 first contact, 2 discovery, 3 education and early evaluation, 4 deep evaluation, 5 internal justification, 6 negotiation and close, 7 handoff to customer success. Fill gaps in a coverage matrix (personas by stages) by frequency and stage. One location, file names that show type, persona, and stage, and a one-page guide.
9. Set governance: a named owner per asset type, a 20-minute monthly gap review with the head of sales, and a retirement rule (unused 6 months, replaced, or inaccurate: archive, do not delete). Review the deck quarterly and after any significant launch; one-pagers every 6 months; case studies yearly and retire at 24 months; ROI calculator yearly; rival content quarterly and within 5 business days of a rival move.
10. Onboarding: a 90-minute library walkthrough in week one and a one-card stage guide.

**Decision rules**
- If you are seed stage, then the deck, one champion one-pager, and a one-page written talk track of the founder's pitch. That page becomes the first hire's onboarding.
- If deals stall at internal justification, then deploy the economic buyer one-pager, ROI calculator, and business case template as a set: one-pager when the economic buyer is named, calculator when they engage, template when the champion asks for help. Expect better stage 4 to 5 conversion in 60 to 90 days.
- If a library exists but new reps do not use it, then fix training and organization, not content.
- If reps build their own materials, then do not mandate the library yet. Ask three of them what their version has that the official one lacks. A real gap gets an asset. "I like my style" is an adoption problem.
- If many assets are never used, then retire before you produce. A smaller, fully used library beats a large, partly used one.
- If enterprise deals stall at technical evaluation, then build an architecture overview, a security FAQ, an integration guide, and an implementation document.
- If you are considering a sales enablement platform, then wait until about 15 reps, 50 active assets, or a need for deal-level tracking.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Reps using a library asset in the last 30 days | 70% or more at Tier 2; under 50% is a findability or relevance problem | Lintel guidance |
| ROI calculator use in stage 4 and later deals | 40 to 60% at Tier 2; under 30% means findability or quality | Lintel guidance |
| Proposal win rate vs overall | At or above; 10 points below usually means proposals go out before the economic buyer is engaged | Lintel guidance |
| Rep hours a week building content | Under 1; over 3 means the library misses real needs | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** A 45-slide deck every rep edits before each call. A "one-pager" that runs two pages. An ROI calculator with a fixed output the rep cannot trace (the economic buyer asks how you got it). Case studies that open with the vendor's story. A proposal template nobody must follow, so proposals differ by rep tenure.

## Launches and launch levels  (FW-PM-003)

**When this matters:** Several updates shipped and none produced measurable pipeline. A major release, pricing change, or market move is within 60 to 90 days with no plan. Sales learns about launches when the market does. Win/loss shows deals a new feature would have saved, but the rep did not know it existed.
**Skip it when:** You are pre-PMF and the product changes weekly (launches are experiments). You have fewer than 5 customers and the founder tells each one personally. Positioning is unstable. This block covers launches to buyers you already sell to. For new buyers, validate the entry in FW-EMERGING-012 first. Press and analyst work is FW-PR-001.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: A blog post and a customer email. No pipeline goal. Sales finds out from the post.
- Tier 2 Building: Three launch levels defined. Significant and Major launches have a pipeline goal and a briefing before announcement.
- Tier 3 Operating: A repeatable playbook with a goal, a sales readiness gate, CRM tagging, and a retrospective that feeds the next plan.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Past launch data sets the size of the next launch, the channel mix, and the order of releases on the roadmap.

**First build, in order**
1. Write the level model on one page (2 hours). Minor: bug fixes and small changes with no new capability. Communication only, under 4 hours of marketing time, no goal. Significant: a capability that matters to one buyer segment or answers a known objection. Targeted outreach, sales content update, segment email, post. 1 to 2 weeks, goal set before launch, sales briefed first. Major: a new product or line, a major pricing or packaging change, or anything that changes what you can credibly say to buyers who said no before. Full playbook, 4 to 8 weeks of preparation, goal and review required. Check levels with the heads of product and sales.
2. Classify the next 3 planned releases (1 hour). Any Significant or Major release needs a goal and a sales plan now, even six weeks out. Starting early is the most reliable improvement a company can make.
3. For the first Significant launch, use six items: pipeline goal written down, target segment named, sales briefed before announcement, customer email separate from the prospect email, a follow-up plan for the 7 days after, and the review booked.
4. For Major launches, use eight parts: strategy document (what, for whom, why now, what success looks like at 90 days); a quantified pipeline goal (for example, 20 new qualified opportunities from one named segment within 60 days); audience definition; channel plan with owners; sales readiness checklist; customer communication plan; market communication plan; and a review date set before launch.
5. Build four audience lists: prospects likely to value it, customers likely to expand, churned customers who left over this gap, and customers of rivals whose weakness this addresses.
6. Make sales readiness a gate: training in buyer-outcome terms, demo, deck, card, and objection guide updated, and a role-play where each rep explains the launch without reading. Announce only after 90% or more of the reps who will sell it pass. If under 90%, delay or limit selling it to those who passed.
7. Customers hear first, and key accounts hear from a person (CSM or AE), not a marketing email. Cover what changes for them and any upgrade path. Brief all customer-facing roles 48 or more hours before the announcement.
8. Build the follow-up before launch day. Days 1 to 3: SDR outreach to engaged priority accounts. Days 3 to 7: retarget visitors to the launch page. Days 5 to 10: CSM follow-up. Day 14: second email to opened-not-replied. Days 14 to 30: sustained content.
9. Tag every launch-created or advanced opportunity in the CRM at creation (launch name, level, channel), not after.
10. Hold a 60-minute retrospective 30 days out, booked before launch: pipeline against goal, channel results, sales readiness, customer response, and one named change to the next plan. Same agenda for wins and misses.

**Decision rules**
- If you are seed stage, then every significant update is a launch and the channel is the founder: personal emails to customers and prospects who care, a short post in buyer-outcome terms, and a mention in the next demos. The only goal is "did this help close or keep deals."
- If you are Series A with a significant launch and no system, then do the minimum: classify it, set a goal, brief sales before announcement, and write a separate customer email. Skip the full eight parts the first time.
- If past launches missed pipeline, then the cause is almost always no goal and no readiness gate. Set a rough goal for the next launch today and book the briefing. With no history, set a directional goal and call it a first-cycle estimate.
- If one marketer faces a Major launch, then form a micro-team. Product owns the strategy document and demo update. A sales manager owns readiness. A CSM lead owns customer communication. The marketer owns channels, content, follow-up, and the retrospective.
- If the readiness gate was bypassed in the last two launches, then it is a governance problem. Take it to the heads of product, marketing, and sales and restore it as a hard rule.
- If ACV is over $50K and you are Series B or later, then add personal AE outreach to the top 20 to 30 named accounts, with its own pipeline target.
- If you have almost no budget, then use personal emails, a blog post, founder posts, and direct outreach to the top 20 relevant prospects. No paid media.
- If you are PLG, then the in-product notice and activation path come before the blog post, and the goal is trial-to-paid lift.
- If a rival announces something that overlaps a planned launch, then assess within 48 hours whether yours is stronger, equal, or weaker, and whether timing should move up, stay, or slip.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Readiness pass rate before announcement | 90% or more | Lintel guidance |
| Major launch pipeline at Series B | 15 to 40 new opportunities within 90 days; under 10 means it was a communication event | Lintel guidance. Rough check; depends on ACV and installed base |
| Feature complete to live | Minor 1 to 3 days; Significant 7 to 14; Major 28 to 42 | Lintel guidance. Longer means the process is too heavy |
| Follow-up inside 72 hours | 15 to 30% of engaged prospects become a qualified conversation; under 10% means slow or generic follow-up | Lintel guidance |
| Customer expansion share of launch pipeline | 30 to 50% with a healthy installed base; under 20% means a thin customer track | Lintel guidance |
| Budget per launch | Seed $0 to $500. Series A $1,000 to $8,000 Significant, $5,000 to $20,000 Major. Series B $3,000 to $15,000 and $15,000 to $60,000 | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** The headline is the feature name, so a prospect cannot tell if it is for them (lead with the buyer's problem). The announcement ends on day one and 70 to 80% of interested prospects go cold. Marketing hears one to two weeks ahead and is told to "write the blog post." The builder writes the copy. Three unrelated features are bundled and none gets a clear message. Reps use every launch to email the whole database, so prospects stop opening.

## Pricing and packaging communication  (FW-PM-003, FW-PM-001, FW-PM-004, FW-PM-005)

**When this matters:** You are changing a price, adding or removing a plan, or moving features between plans. A rival changed theirs. Losses cite price and you need to know whether it is price or a missing value story. Reps need a pricing guide and discount rules.
**Skip it when:** The question is what to charge, how to package, or whether to change price at all. That is FW-GROWTH-005, with the cost check in finance. This block covers telling people, training sales, and judging whether price is the real problem. The library is thin here, so this block stays within what it states.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: A pricing change goes out as a post or email. Customers and reps learn when the market does.
- Tier 2 Building: Pricing and packaging changes count as Major launches. Customers hear first and reps are trained before announcement.
- Tier 3 Operating: A pricing guide and discount rules exist for the close stage. Price losses are checked with buyers before price moves.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Launch data and win/loss price findings are a standing input to pricing decisions made under FW-GROWTH-005.

**First build, in order**
1. Classify the change as a Major launch and start 4 to 8 weeks out.
2. Extend the positioning document to cover the change before anything goes outside, or the message will conflict with the rest of your story.
3. Tell customers before the market. Key accounts hear from their CSM or AE in person. Cover what changes for them, what they must do, and any upgrade path, separate from the public message. If the customer message went out the same day as the public one or later in the last three launches, the customer track is missing.
4. Pass the readiness gate. Write the pricing guide for the negotiation stage with approval rules for discounts and a contract FAQ. Proposals pull their price section from that guide.
5. Set a pipeline goal, tag opportunities, and book the 30-day retrospective.

**Decision rules**
- If losses center on price, then do not touch price first. Interview 5 buyers on what they compared it to, check the ROI calculator, and check whether the economic buyer heard the value story.
- If a rival changes price, then update the price comparison on their card within 5 business days and brief sales within a week. Whether to change yours is FW-GROWTH-005's call.
- If a major pricing decision is coming and you have no win/loss data for the segment, then interview 5 to 8 buyers in 3 to 4 weeks first.

**Where it goes wrong:** Customers learn of the change from a post or a prospect's forwarded email (enterprise accounts become churn and escalation risks). Price gets blamed for losses that are really missing value stories. Reps meet the new price in a call before they were trained. Proposals quote a price that differs from the guide.

## Roadmap feedback loop  (FW-PM-001, FW-PM-004, FW-PM-006)

**When this matters:** Win/loss or rival work keeps showing the same product gap. Product says nobody told them. Roadmap debates run on opinion.
**Skip it when:** You have under 10 closed deals, or you are pre-PMF and the product changes weekly. Product marketing supplies intelligence and does not set the roadmap.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: Product builds on its own judgment. Marketing hears about releases from the changelog.
- Tier 2 Building: Product marketing is consulted now and then, with no set channel.
- Tier 3 Operating: A quarterly presentation of win/loss and rival gaps to product, plus a standing agenda item in roadmap planning.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Roadmap decisions cite buyer research by name. Commercial impact shapes release order.

**First build, in order**
1. Get a standing slot in quarterly roadmap planning and name who presents.
2. Bring three inputs: product findings from win/loss (High confidence only), a rival gap list ranked by frequency and deal value, and unmet needs from buyer research.
3. Sort gaps into deal-blocking (you lack it and lose over it), parity (investing creates a difference), and advantage (investing widens the lead). Attach one action to each: build it, position it differently, or accept it and give sales a response.
4. Quantify: "we lost X deals last quarter citing F, worth $Y in pipeline." Give product a ranked list, not a report. Record in the roadmap decision record whether each input was accepted. Product need not accept all of it, but the input must be documented.
5. Run a monthly product-marketing sync that classifies upcoming releases by launch level and checks preparation.

**Decision rules**
- If a feature gap appears in 30% or more of competitive losses, then it is a product gap. Take it to product within 2 weeks with deals lost and pipeline value, and give sales a bridging line meanwhile: name the gap honestly, say the plan, move to your strengths.
- If the same product finding returns for 3 or more quarters with no response, then skip the normal queue. Annualize the revenue at risk and go to the CEO and head of product.
- If no input was acknowledged in two quarters, then find the break: not captured in interviews, not reaching product, or not acknowledged. Fix that one.
- If product distrusts the intelligence, then check whether it is grounded in deal data and whether it arrived as a long report. Switch to ranked gaps with High, Medium, or Low confidence.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Roadmap decisions per quarter citing product marketing output | 1 to 2 at Tier 3; zero means the loop is broken | Lintel guidance |
| A finding recurring after action | At most once in the next two quarters | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** Long reports go to product. No standing agenda slot exists. Single data points are presented as patterns. Roadmap fights run on opinion because nobody brings quantified gaps.

## Category creation: the readiness test  (FW-PM-007)

**When this matters:** Buyers describe your problem in improvised words because no category name exists, and they have to invent a budget line to buy you. Analysts and press compare you to products that do not solve the same problem. A rival is naming the category and will set the buying criteria. You have clear fit in one segment and executives committed for 18 to 24 months.
**Skip it when:** You are before Series B. At seed and Series A, run the test and invest in nothing else. Most companies that think they need a category have a positioning problem inside an existing one, which is faster and cheaper to fix (see the positioning block). Choosing a category path is FW-EMERGING-005; analyst briefings are FW-INFLUENCE-001; press is FW-PR-001.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: Describing yourself in a category others defined.
- Tier 2 Building: A preferred category name and a first narrative.
- Tier 3 Operating: A sustained content, community, and analyst program around the problem.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Buyers, press, and analysts use your category words unprompted.

**First build, in order**
1. Run the four-question readiness test with outside input, not founders alone. (a) If a buyer bought the leader of the nearest existing category, would the problem stay meaningfully unsolved? (b) When 5 buyers describe the problem, do they improvise ("sort of like X but not really") or reach for an existing name? (c) Can you sustain 18 to 24 months of category work next to the core business? That takes 0.5 to 1.0 FTE of PMM or content lead and 4 to 8 CEO hours a month. (d) Is there a well-funded rival with a 12-month head start on the same claim? All four must come out in favor. Any miss means lead the nearest existing category instead. Question (a) settles most cases.
2. If you pass, list 3 to 5 names. Judge each on whether buyers recognize it with little explanation, whether a rival could claim it without your approach, and whether it still fits in five years. Ask 5 buyers and 2 analysts "when I say this name, what kind of product comes to mind." Pick the most consistent, intended answer, not the cleverest name.
3. Confirm the CEO backs the 24-month spend, not only the name.
4. Write the narrative in four parts: the enemy (the old way as a paradigm, not a named rival), the problem it creates (quantified), the new way (describable without your product name), and the promise. Publish nothing until steps 1 to 3 are done.

**Decision rules**
- If you are seed or Series A, then run the test and stop. A founder with a big audience can seed the language through personal channels at low cost, but skip analyst relations and community infrastructure.
- If category work competes with demand generation, then pipeline comes first when it is short. At Series B keep category spend to about 20 to 25% of marketing budget at most.
- If a well-funded rival named the category 12 or more months before you, then argue your version is incomplete, or lead inside theirs and win customers there.

**Numbers that matter**
| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Buyers using your category words unprompted | Year 1 0 to 5%; year 2 15 to 25%; year 3 and later 35 to 50% | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** The category is announced before the readiness test and the team, not buyers, picks the name. The CEO backs the name but not the long spend, so the program is defunded after one weak quarter. The manifesto is a product pitch. The community centers on the vendor's product, not the problem.
