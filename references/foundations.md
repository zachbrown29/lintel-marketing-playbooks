# Foundations

The questions a B2B software company answers before spending on any channel: who to sell to, why they pick you, what to say, how you acquire, how much to spend, what to build first, and what to refuse. For a founder or marketer at a seed to Series B company.

**Owns:** ICP definition and tiering, buyer personas and the buying committee, competitive positioning and category choice, messaging architecture, which go-to-market motion to run, marketing budget size and allocation by stage, annual and 90-day planning, how to decide what not to do, and the state a company should be in before the first marketing hire.

**Does not own:** Running a channel once the foundation is set (demand gen, content, SEO, paid, outbound, events, lifecycle). Sales org design, the first rep, qualification methods, pipeline stages (FW-SALES-001, FW-SALES-003). Measurement infrastructure and funnel definitions (FW-FOUND-006). Stack architecture and tool choice (FW-FOUND-007, FW-OPS-003, FW-REVOPS-002). Price levels and packaging math.

## ICP and segmentation (FW-FOUND-001)

An ICP describes a company type, not a person. "Mid-market B2B SaaS" is a market category, not an ICP. Test: if it cannot decide whether a specific company is in or out in under 60 seconds, it is not functional.

**When this matters:** Wins and losses follow no pattern anyone can explain. Marketing produces volume and sales calls it the wrong volume. You are about to spend on paid, outbound, ABM, or content with no written target. A marketing hire is starting with no ICP to inherit. You have customers and have never asked which are best and why.

**Skip it when:** Fewer than five paying customers, so a premature ICP narrows prospecting before any pattern exists. The product is still changing significantly during product-market fit discovery. An ICP from the last 12 months was checked against closed-won data and is actually used by sales and marketing.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: Nothing written. People describe the best customer differently. Sales chases everything.
- Tier 2 Building: A written ICP exists but was never tested against win/loss data, and sales overrides it for big off-ICP deals.
- Tier 3 Operating: ICP-fit accounts demonstrably close better. A scoring rubric runs on inbound, a negative ICP screens bad fits, review is quarterly.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Every CRM account carries a fit score that refreshes with enrichment. Marketing, sales, and customer success allocate by tier, and the model weights are re-fit against outcomes.

**First build, in order**
1. Define "best customer" on three dimensions before pulling data: high ACV or lifetime revenue, low CAC, and high NRR. Revenue alone produces an ICP built around your hardest customer.
2. Export every paying customer and rank on those three. The top third is the reference set. Ten is enough to analyze, five is the floor. 2 hours.
3. Open the top third's sites and LinkedIn pages. Record headcount, industry, geography, revenue, funding stage, tech stack, and whether they have the function your product serves. Keep the three to five traits common in the top third and uncommon in the bottom third. 2 hours.
4. Interview five of them: what were you trying to accomplish, what did you try before, what made you decide. This surfaces the job the product gets hired for plus triggers no database holds (fast growth, a new Head of X, a new round).
5. Write one page: summary, firmographic criteria with thresholds, technographic signals, behavioral signals, negative ICP, and the data basis. Label it Version 1 and date it. 1 hour.
6. Validate with whoever closes deals using the last five wins and five losses. Do the criteria explain the pattern? 1 hour.
7. Score the 5 to 10 most active open deals and show sales the ranking. Agreement means it works. 30 minutes.
8. Only then operationalize: CRM score, a named owner, and a 60-minute quarterly review on the calendar.

**Decision rules**
- If under five paying customers, build a hypothesis ICP from two or three, label it a hypothesis, and review every five new deals. No enrichment tooling or CRM scoring until it is tested against 15 deals.
- If an ICP exists but was never checked against win/loss data, validation is the whole job. Score the last 20 to 30 closed deals.
- If ICP-fit deals win 15+ points more often, operationalize. Under 10 points, rebuild the criteria from the data.
- If the founder runs sales with no marketing team, cap it at three to five criteria answerable from a LinkedIn page in 60 seconds. The point is a tool for saying no.
- If one customer pays 40% of revenue, use them as a case study, not the ICP anchor. One customer is a data point.
- If a predictive criterion cannot be bought from any data source, substitute a sourceable proxy (a job title standing in for "has a RevOps function") in the scoring model and keep the original in the strategy doc.
- If you sell two products to different buyers, build two ICPs. A blended ICP describes neither.
- If entering a new country, run the home ICP as a hypothesis for 20 deals. Expect roughly 60 to 70% to transfer.
- Include customer success in the build. They hold the retention knowledge the third dimension depends on.

**Numbers that matter**

| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Win rate by tier | Tier 1 30-50%, Tier 2 15-25%, Tier 3 5-12% | Lintel guidance. Tier 1 should be 2x to 4x Tier 3 |
| Tier 1 vs Tier 2 win rate | At least 50% higher | Within 10 points means the tiers are not differentiated |
| Tier 1 share of pipeline value | 40%+ mid-market and enterprise, 30% SMB | Under 20% means effort sits on low-probability accounts |
| 12-month churn by tier | Tier 1 under 5%, Tier 2 under 12%, Tier 3 under 20% | Tier 1 above 8% means the ICP predicts buying, not success |
| ACV by tier | Tier 1 1.5x to 3x Tier 2, 3x to 6x Tier 3 | Lower means size or budget signals are missing |
| Time to close by tier | Tier 1 same or faster than Tier 2 | 30%+ slower means procurement complexity is unscreened |
| Fit score bands | 75-100 Tier 1, 50-74 Tier 2, 25-49 inbound only, under 25 disqualify | Negative ICP subtracts 20 points when three or more signals fire |
| Build cost, seed | 5-8 hours, then 1-2 hours a month, under $200/month tooling | Lintel guidance |
| Build cost, Series A | 20-30 hours, then 3-5 hours a month, $500-1,500/month tooling | Outside facilitation $3,000-8,000, owned internally |

**Where it goes wrong:** Rebuilding from scratch instead of replacing the two or three criteria that do not correlate, discarding validated negative-ICP signals. Leaving customer success out, so retention criteria are guesses. Confusing ICP tier with deal priority, so reps chase Tier 1 accounts that are not in market. A good document SDRs and CSMs never see.

## Buyer personas and the buying committee (FW-FOUND-002)

The ICP says which company. The persona says which humans, what triggers them, what they fear, and how they decide.

**When this matters:** The ICP is validated but content is not landing and prospects do not see themselves in the messaging. Deals die at qualification or demo. You are about to invest in content, paid social, or outbound email. A new use case means a new buyer. Churn analysis shows the buyer and the user are different people.

**Skip it when:** The ICP is not defined, so personas get built for the wrong company type. Fewer than 10 customers and sales is entirely founder-led. Personas from the last 12 months were checked against real calls and are in use.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: No written personas. Each rep carries a private mental model. Marketing writes for "our audience."
- Tier 2 Building: A champion persona exists. Economic buyer, end user, and skeptic are undocumented, so multi-stakeholder deals get improvised.
- Tier 3 Operating: All four roles documented and validated. Reps multi-thread on purpose, objection handling is persona-specific, personas are tagged on key CRM contacts.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Persona tags drive content, sequencing, and routing. Win rate and velocity report by persona combination and order of engagement. Call recordings are mined continuously.

**First build, in order**
1. List every job title in the last 10 closed-won deals, marking champion, budget approver, and anyone else involved. 1 hour.
2. Audit before researching. Review call recordings from the last 30 deals, won and lost, for repeated words, worries, and objections. Teams that audit first typically find 60 to 70% of the material already exists unstructured.
3. Interview three customers, reasonably satisfied rather than loudest fans. Ask: what made you start looking, what worried you most, what almost stopped you, what tipped it, what would you tell someone like you. Record it; verbatim language is the raw material. 3 hours.
4. Write the one-page champion persona: role, trigger, problem in their words, top fears in their words, success criteria, decision process, and the one message that moves them. 2 hours.
5. Validate with the rep who has had the most buyer conversations. Ask what it misses about the real conversation. 1 hour.
6. Cut a 60-second cheat sheet. That is the operational tool; the full document is the reference. 1 hour.
7. Only then build the economic buyer persona: risk, ROI, what they need to approve, and what makes them say no.

**Decision rules**
- Champion first, always. Four shallow personas are worse than one good one.
- If deals are above $15,000 ACV, qualify for the economic buyer in Stage 1 and engage them before the end of Stage 2. They are the most common deal-killer outside the champion.
- If the persona came only from happy customers, it is incomplete. Fears and objections must come from lost deals and call recordings.
- If the same title plays different roles across deals, stop mapping persona to title. Ask in every deal what role this person plays.
- If a $5,000 deal and a $50,000 deal have different approval paths, write two champion personas. One covering both is accurate for neither.
- If the product adds a new primary use case or drops a major capability, trigger an out-of-cycle persona review.
- If an inbound lead matches the champion profile but the company fails the ICP, it is not a deal.

**Numbers that matter**

| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Five Rings completeness | 8-10 decision-grade, 5-7 gaps, 3-4 a profile not a persona, 0-2 rebuild | Five Rings of Buying Insight, Adele Revella, Buyer Persona Institute |
| Buying committee coverage | 8-10 of 10 fully mapped, under 5 means champion only | Champion, economic buyer, end user, skeptic, influencer, 2 points each |
| Win rate lift, champion plus economic buyer by Stage 2 | 30-50% higher than champion only, ACV above $20,000 | Lintel guidance. Under 10% means tracking or the conversation is wrong |
| Velocity lift, full committee by Stage 2 | 20-35% faster than sequential Stage 3 engagement | Lintel guidance |
| Discovery advance rate | Champion 55-70%, economic buyer first meeting 65-80% | Champion under 40% means discovery is miscalibrated |
| Stage 3 loss rate | 2-3x higher without economic buyer engagement before Stage 3 | Lintel guidance |
| Build cost, seed | 8-15 hours for the champion persona, under $500, 30-45 minutes a month | Lintel guidance |
| Build cost, Series A | 40-60 hours for champion plus economic buyer, $500-2,000, 10-15 hours per half-year review | Outside interview help $2,000-5,000; synthesis stays internal |

**Where it goes wrong:** Treating the champion as the decision maker, which optimizes everything for the person who cannot say yes. Building from your most articulate customers, who are self-selected advocates and under-represent real skepticism and politics. Describing the buyer at their best instead of the version who is busy and arguing against the purchase. Never updating after a product change.

## Positioning and category choice (FW-FOUND-003)

Positioning is the strategic choice of which buyers, which category, and which differentiated value. Messaging is the words. Weak positioning produces weak messaging no matter how good the writer is, so diagnose which is broken first.

**When this matters:** Reps give different answers to "why you over them?" Messaging lists features and never explains why this buyer should choose you. You are about to launch a product, enter a market, or widen the ICP. A funded competitor changed their messaging and win rates moved. You passed 20 employees and several people describe the product differently.

**Skip it when:** The ICP is not validated, since position is relative to a specific buyer and a specific set of alternatives. Fewer than 10 customers and you are still learning what the product is best at. Positioning from the last 12 months with no competitor movement and stable win rates.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: Nothing written. Competitive comparison is improvised per rep. No battlecards.
- Tier 2 Building: A document exists and shows up in at least one place on the site. Battlecards may cover one or two competitors, unvalidated and inconsistently used.
- Tier 3 Operating: Position is stable and used across marketing and sales. Battlecards cover all major competitors, refreshed quarterly. Competitive win rate is tracked separately.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Intelligence runs continuously and position shifts before competitor moves hit win rates. Category strategy is managed deliberately. Competitive win rate is a board metric.

**First build, in order**
1. List the alternatives. Pull the last 20 deals and record every competitor named in notes, loss reasons, or conversations. Add the status quo. Map the set by buyer segment, since an enterprise buyer and a seed buyer consider different options. 2 hours.
2. Find genuine unique attributes. Build a capability table from competitor feature pages and review profiles. Keep only what you have and the top one or two competitors meaningfully lack. 2 hours.
3. Interview three customers: what made you choose us, and what would have been hardest to replace if we did not exist. Do not skip this under time pressure. 2 hours.
4. Write one page on the five components: competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value to this buyer, target market characteristics, market category. One to three sentences each. 1 hour.
5. Validate with the two reps who ran the most competitive deals. 1 hour.
6. Then build battlecards for the top two or three competitors: their positioning, their strengths as buyers see them, where and why they win, and the counter-narratives. Run each past five reps first.
7. For sentiment, read the last 50 reviews of each competitor on G2 or Capterra. Repeated praise and complaints beat the competitor's self-description.

**Decision rules**
- If your top three competitors can honestly make the same claim, it is table stakes and cannot carry primary positioning. Ease of use, fast implementation, and great support are the usual offenders.
- If you are Series A and positioning primarily against the largest player, stop. Position against the competitors that actually appear in your deals.
- If the positioning needs three paragraphs of internal explanation, it is not clear enough to work outside the building.
- If you are defining a new category before you have customer density, media presence, and product proof, compete inside the nearest existing category first. Early category creation produces very long cycles and founder-dependent deals. Most companies that later created a category competed in an existing one for years.
- If one loss reason exceeds 40% of competitive losses, that is systemic. Price means value positioning fails, features mean the attribute claims do not land, trust means thin proof, brand means a category or presence problem.
- If positioning has no stance on price, it is incomplete. Priced above competitors needs a premium justification; priced below needs to avoid reading as cheap.
- Never publish the internal positioning document. Translate it.

**Numbers that matter**

| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Positioning audit score | 8-10 strong, 5-7 close gaps first, 3-4 rebuild, 0-2 start from zero | April Dunford's Obviously Awesome five components |
| Competitive win rate | Within 10 points of overall; 35-50% against direct competitors is healthy early | 15+ points below overall means positioning underperforms |
| Win rate by competitor | Stable or improving quarter over quarter | An 8-point decline over two quarters triggers an immediate battlecard rebuild |
| Loss reason concentration | No single reason above 30% of competitive losses | Above 40% is a systemic gap |
| Build cost, seed | 10-20 hours, then 1-2 hours a month, under $500 | No intelligence tooling needed yet |
| Build cost, Series A | 20-30 hours, then 4-6 hours a month, $500-2,000 | Consultant $5,000-15,000 if used; validation stays internal |
| Category creation investment | $500,000-1,500,000 a year, appropriate above roughly $20M ARR | Lintel guidance. Category leaders command 20-40% price premiums |

**Where it goes wrong:** Writing the tagline before the strategy. Building the comparison from your feature list instead of the buyer's evaluation criteria, which turns positioning into a feature fight. Claiming to beat everyone on everything, which no buyer believes. Positioning the company's pedigree instead of the product. Letting battlecards go stale until a win-rate drop forces the update.

## Messaging architecture (FW-FOUND-004)

The internal source document that makes a cold email, a homepage, a sales deck, and a conference talk tell the same story in the same language.

**When this matters:** Marketing and sales describe the product differently, so prospects get two stories. You are about to produce a lot of content, launch paid media, or hire several people who speak externally. Traffic is reasonable and site conversion is not. Positioning is done and has never been turned into language.

**Skip it when:** Positioning is not built, so there is nothing to translate. The ICP is not validated. An architecture from the last 12 months is in use with conversion and reply rates at or above benchmark.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: No document. The site was written by whoever built it, sales uses its own language, every content piece starts from scratch.
- Tier 2 Building: A roughly right core message exists, but site, deck, and outbound each run a different version.
- Tier 3 Operating: Core message, proof messages, and channel adaptations documented and used. Content comes from briefs. New hires get messaging training. The proof library is maintained.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Core messages A/B tested on a cadence, architecture updated on performance rather than taste, personalization delivering persona-specific messaging at scale.

**First build, in order**
1. Write the core message: "We help [specific company type] [achieve specific outcome] by [specific mechanism]." Read it aloud to someone who fits the buyer. "That sounds like us" means it works. "Interesting, but what does that mean in practice" means the mechanism is vague. 1 hour.
2. Write three proof messages, one per top unique attribute. Format: specific claim, then specific proof. Each needs a number, a source, and the buyer outcome. Mark missing proof TBD and treat collecting it as a priority. 2 hours.
3. Write three objection responses from the top three fears in the persona research: acknowledge, then redirect to specific proof. These are messages for the FAQ, nurture, or talk track, not sales scripts. 1 hour.
4. Write one call to action per stage. Awareness gets the lightest ask, decision the heaviest. Mismatch the ask to the stage and conversion stays low regardless of message quality. 1 hour.
5. Validate twice: a rep ("is this what you actually say?") and an ICP-fit customer or prospect ("does this sound like a problem you have?"). Act on real misalignment, not word preferences. 1 hour.
6. Write the tagline last.

**Decision rules**
- If you are about to hire a copywriter or agency, build the architecture first. Most "messaging problems" are architectural, not executional.
- If there are more than five primary messages, there are none. One core message, three to five proof messages, the rest supporting detail.
- If a quantified claim has no externally shareable proof asset, pull the claim. A claim that collapses when a buyer asks for evidence is worse than silence.
- If you serve two ICPs with genuinely different problems, build a variant per ICP rather than averaging them.
- If the team says the architecture limits creativity, restate the scope: it fixes what to say and leaves how to say it open.
- If the product gains a significant capability, review whether a new proof message belongs.

**Numbers that matter**

| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| StoryBrand clarity score | 8-10 buyer-centric, 5-7 partial, 3-4 rewrite, 0-2 start from zero | Donald Miller, Building a StoryBrand (2017) |
| Message hierarchy score | 7-8 of 8 functional, 3-4 needs prioritization, 0-2 full rebuild | Tests recall, ranking, proof attached, stage mapping |
| Homepage conversion | Self-serve sign-up 3-6% of unique visitors; sales-led demo booking 1-3% | Below 1% sales-led means the message is not connecting |
| Cold email reply rate, ICP-fit | 3-6%; above 8% exceptional | Below 2% means the opening line is product-centric, not trigger-centric |
| Sales deck engagement | 70%+ open rate, 7+ of 12 slides viewed by an engaged prospect | Near-zero dwell slides are not delivering their message |
| Build cost, seed | 8-12 hours, then 1-2 hours a month, under $500 | Lintel guidance |
| Build cost, Series A | 20-30 hours, then 4-6 hours a month, 6 hours per quarterly review | $1,000-5,000 if a writer does the first pass; $500-2,000 per case study, target three in six months |

**Where it goes wrong:** Starting with the tagline. Fifteen co-equal primary messages, so the buyer holds none. Writing only for sales or only for marketing, so the gap between them undermines both. Claims whose proof is a single customer or an unpublishable case study.

## GTM motion choice and design (FW-FOUND-005)

A motion is a repeatable, documented sequence any trained person can run that produces predictable results. A collection of activities is not a motion. Test: can you describe how a new hire acquires their first five customers in 30 days?

**When this matters:** A first real sales or marketing investment with no motion chosen. Deals close but the pattern is unclear and pipeline cannot be forecast. You are about to scale a sales team that needs something to train on. The ICP moved upmarket or into a new vertical. You are weighing a second motion, such as an enterprise overlay on a product-led base.

**Skip it when:** The ICP is not validated. Fewer than 10 customers and still in product-market fit discovery. A motion is clearly working with healthy pipeline and consistent execution. Optimize instead.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: Customers come from the founder's network, referrals, and whatever arrives. No documented process, no stages, no forecast.
- Tier 2 Building: The primary motion is named and documented at a high level. New hires have a basic playbook needing heavy coaching. Conversion rates are known roughly.
- Tier 3 Operating: Fully documented, consistently run, predictable. New hires ramp inside the target window. Unit economics are healthy and understood, and the motion changes each quarter on conversion data.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Multiple coordinated motions, real-time stage tracking, experiments on individual motion elements, proactive redesign when the market shifts.

**First build, in order**
1. Pull the last 15 to 20 closed-won deals. Per deal: how you became aware of them, who initiated, days to close, ACV, and whether they still pay and have expanded. This sheet is the motion you already run. 2 hours.
2. Name the dominant pattern. Which source produced the most deals, and which produced the best outcomes by retention and expansion? No cluster means you need more deals before the pattern is readable. 1 hour.
3. Write one page: who initiates, the first action, the stages to close, who is involved at each, how long each takes, and stage-to-stage conversion. Specific enough that a new hire knows what to do in week one. 1 hour.
4. Calculate rough unit economics. CAC equals trailing 12-month sales and marketing spend divided by customers acquired. Payback equals CAC divided by monthly revenue per customer. Rough beats absent. 1 hour.
5. Test it on two people: your best closer ("does this match how deals get won?") and someone who has never closed here ("could you start tomorrow from this?"). 1 hour.
6. Then instrument CRM stages to match the motion, since conversion cannot be optimized before it is measured.

**Decision rules**
- ACV above $25,000 fits sales-led. Below $5,000 fits product-led or low-touch. $5,000 to $25,000 fits inside sales. Enterprise field sales on a $3,000 product is a severe mismatch.
- If value lands in under 30 minutes, offer a trial or free tier. If implementation takes 60+ days, run a high-touch guided process. A self-serve product gated behind a sales call, or a 90-day implementation sold with no onboarding, both score zero on fit.
- If purchases need committee approval, the motion has to engage the committee deliberately.
- If going product-led and under 30% of sign-ups reach the aha moment, fix the product experience before spending on the channel.
- If outbound reply rate is under 2% and CAC is above benchmark, it is a targeting problem. Cut the bottom 30% of accounts by ICP score, require a timing signal (recent funding, new executive, technology change), and run 30 days before changing anything else.
- If inbound-led at Series B with a pipeline constraint and under roughly 2,000 ICP-fit organic visitors a month for a mid-market product, inbound lacks reach. Add outbound or serious content investment.
- Do not hire before the motion is documented. Close 20 deals, find the pattern, write it down, then hire to run it.
- If one or two reps outperform, formalize their replicable techniques, not their charm or network.
- If a stage is broken, fix the stage. Do not redesign the motion.

**Numbers that matter**

| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| GTM fit score | 80-100 strong, 60-79 fix sub-12 dimensions before scaling, 40-59 redesign, under 40 mismatch | Scores ACV fit, time-to-value fit, buyer fit, channel efficiency, teachability |
| CAC payback | Under 12 months excellent, 12-18 healthy, 18-24 manageable if NRR above 110%, over 24 act | Lintel guidance |
| LTV:CAC | 3:1 minimum, 4:1 healthy, 5:1+ may mean underinvestment in growth | Lintel guidance |
| Magic number | Above 0.75, above 1.0 excellent | Net new quarterly ARR x 4 ÷ prior quarter S&M spend |
| CAC by channel | Inbound 20-40% below outbound; product-led 50-70% below sales-led | Outbound above 2.5x inbound means audit targeting |
| Stage conversion, inside sales | Discovery to demo 65-80%, demo to proposal 55-70%, proposal to close 35-50%, end to end 15-25% | Lintel guidance |
| Sales cycle, inside sales to mid-market | 20-45 days healthy, 45-90 moderate, above 90 signals complexity or a qualification gap | Lintel guidance |
| New hire ramp | Benchmark performance inside 60 days | 90+ days, or founder-dependent closing, scores zero on teachability |
| S&M spend | Seed 20-40% of burn; Series A 40-60% of spend; Series B 35-55% of ARR; Series C+ 30-50% declining | Lintel guidance. Above 80% of ARR at Series A will not scale |

**Where it goes wrong:** Hiring before the motion exists, so each rep invents their own. Copying a reference company's motion without checking it against your ACV and buyer. Designing around one exceptional deal instead of the repeatable 80%. Improving the stage the team enjoys instead of the stage with the biggest gap.

## Marketing budget by stage (FW-FOUND-008)

A spending ceiling is not a budget. A budget framework connects spend to output, allocates with stated reasons, and lets the operator answer "if we cut 20%, what do we lose?" in ten minutes.

**When this matters:** Spend is approved ad hoc with no annual plan. Nobody can connect spend to what it produced. A board or CFO has asked marketing to justify its budget. A new fiscal year is starting. Marketing is at risk of a cut with no framework for defending what matters.

**Skip it when:** Fewer than five customers and no product-market fit. Total spend under $10,000 a year across one or two channels with clear output. A product, team, or financial crisis has to be resolved first.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: Spend exists but is not organized. Last year's total cannot be calculated without real effort.
- Tier 2 Building: An annual budget approved before the year, allocated by category, tracked monthly against actuals, with a named owner and a loose link to a revenue target.
- Tier 3 Operating: Every line has an output metric and a cost per output. Quarterly reallocation produces documented decisions. Cut scenarios exist.
- Tier 4 Compounding: Reforecast quarterly, tied to pipeline coverage and channel CAC, with pre-agreed reallocation triggers, and finance and the board using the efficiency model in growth planning.

**First build, in order**
1. Spend audit. Pull 12 months of marketing expenses from card statements, accounts payable, and contractor agreements into one sheet: date, payee, amount, category, output. Categories: headcount, programs, content and creative, technology, agency and contractors, events. Total by category. 2-4 hours.
2. Attach the best available output metric to each program line, even from memory. 1-2 hours.
3. Build the pipeline model before setting the number. Revenue target, pipeline coverage ratio, divide by average deal size for required opportunities, apply the opportunity conversion rate for the lead target. Without this every budget number is a guess.
4. Set the total from the stage ranges below, then calculate required program spend from the lead target and cost per lead, plus headcount and technology. If it exceeds what leadership will approve, the honest options are a lower revenue target, a specific plan to improve cost per lead, or covering part of the gap with sales-led outbound. 1 hour.
5. Allocate across categories using the stage ratios, writing down the reason for any real deviation. 1 hour.
6. Monthly tracker: budget, actual, variance, plus output metric and target per category. A 30-minute review on a fixed date is enough at this stage. 2-3 hours.
7. Build cut scenarios at 10%, 20%, and 30% with category detail and pipeline impact before anyone asks.

**Decision rules**
- If ARR is under $1M, target 10-30% of ARR. Funded and testing three to five channels sits near the top; bootstrapped with one working channel near the bottom.
- If a program cut above $25,000 is proposed, present the pipeline impact at the moment of the decision. A Q1 cut shows up in Q2 or Q3 and gets blamed on sales execution.
- If budget is challenged, never answer with activity metrics. The minimum credible answer is cost per qualified lead by category plus the pipeline contribution model, which means building that data before the challenge arrives.
- If cost per lead falls, check the lead-to-qualified rate for the same source. A $50 lead at 5% costs more per opportunity than a $200 lead at 30%.
- If the lead target exceeds what sales can work, size the program budget to the volume sales can work, not more.
- If an agency role is more than 20 hours a week of recurring work inside the primary growth motion, move it in-house. A full-time hire typically costs 40-60% of the fully loaded agency cost for the same volume.
- Allocation decisions belong to the budget owner and finance using efficiency data, not to the channel owners whose budgets move.

| Cost per qualified lead | LinkedIn ads $150-400, branded search $30-100, non-branded search $80-250, organic content $50-150, events $200-600, outbound with marketing support $100-300 | Lintel guidance |
| Cost per sales-qualified lead | SMB $500-2,000, mid-market $1,000-5,000, enterprise $3,000-15,000+ | Lintel guidance |
| Qualified-to-sales-accepted rate | 20-30%; under 15% over-production of low quality, above 40% often over-filtering | Lintel guidance |
| Pipeline coverage ratio | 3x-4x typical, 5x-6x enterprise long cycles, 2x-3x high-velocity SMB | Lintel guidance |
| Program spend to pipeline multiple | $5-$10 pipeline per $1 of program spend; below $3 is an efficiency problem | Lintel guidance |
| Budget utilization | 90-105% healthy; under 80% programs are not executing; above 110% controls are not working | Lintel guidance |

**Where it goes wrong:** Defending budget with impressions and posts when leadership asked what it produced. Treating program cuts as free because the pipeline lag hides the cost for two quarters. Planning the lead target without checking sales capacity. Protecting weak channels because cuts read as performance reviews of the teams that own them.

## Planning, priorities, and saying no (FW-FOUND-009)

A channel roadmap is not a strategy. A strategy names what you are trying to accomplish, which constraint is blocking growth, and what you will build or stop to address it.

**When this matters:** A new fiscal year with no written plan and ad hoc quarterly priorities. Marketing and sales hold different pipeline assumptions. The motion is validated and you need a build sequence. Leadership wants a plan. Several functions run with no shared prioritization logic.

**Skip it when:** Product-market fit is unvalidated. The marketing function is under 90 days old. Fewer than three customers, so the constraint diagnostic has nothing to read. Mid-year in execution mode with no out-of-cycle trigger fired.

**Where you are**
- Tier 1 Reactive: No plan. A one-page brief is the right first artifact.
- Tier 2 Building: An annual plan with a pipeline model, category budget allocation, and quarterly milestones for the top three priorities.
- Tier 3 Operating: Planning integrated with sales capacity and finance in one revenue model, with a cross-function prioritization framework and a quarterly replan.
- Tier 4 Compounding: A full integrated cycle starting 8 weeks before the fiscal year, with retrospective, scenario models, and a board-level plan.

**First build, the one-page version**
1. Answer five questions in writing: the 12-month revenue target, how many new customers that requires, where the best customers come from today, the biggest obstacle to reaching more like them, and what marketing actually spends time on. 2 hours.
2. Write the page: what the target requires from marketing, the primary growth motion in one sentence, the top two priorities for 90 days, and one metric per priority. 2 hours.
3. Review with the sales leader and finance before executing. Confirm the implied lead volume matches what sales can work and the implied spend matches the budget. 1 hour.
4. Weekly tracking: week, target, actual, notes. Twenty minutes. Discipline beats sophistication here. 1 hour.
5. Put the 90-day replan on the calendar before starting. 10 minutes.

**Then the full annual version, in order**
1. Retrospective first. Per initiative: was the hypothesis confirmed or refuted, what did it produce against expectation, what would you change. Two to three pages.
2. Constraint diagnostic. Score awareness, consideration, conversion, retention, and expansion 1 to 3 for severity with written evidence. Highest score is the primary constraint.
3. Turn the top one or two constraints into strategic priorities. A priority states an outcome and a measure. "Launch a content program" is an activity. "Raise demo request conversion from organic from 12% to 25% among ICP-fit evaluators" is a priority.
4. Write a growth hypothesis per priority: if we invest in X, we expect Y within Z, assuming A, refuted if B. If you cannot write the refutation condition, the initiative is not defined well enough to fund.
5. Build the pipeline model, derive the budget from it, and map every major line to a hypothesis. Budget lines without a hypothesis are a planning failure.
6. Set binary quarterly milestones. "50 qualified leads per week from paid LinkedIn" is a milestone. "Strong LinkedIn performance" is not. A miss is the scheduled moment to diagnose and adjust.
7. Define per hypothesis a primary metric, a lagging indicator, a leading indicator, and the leading-indicator threshold that triggers an out-of-cycle review.
8. Integration review with sales (lead volume by quarter, lead definition, model assumptions, reporting cadence) and finance (budget model, category approval, efficiency targets, cut scenarios). Log open items with owners and dates.
9. Produce two documents: a one to two page strategic brief for leadership and the working plan for the team. 8 to 12 pages total for Series A to B, readable in 30 minutes.
10. Set the quarterly replan before execution starts. Two hours: hypothesis review, milestone review, plan updates, next quarter priorities.

**Decision rules for saying no**
- No initiative enters the plan without a documented link to a scored constraint. Require the constraint diagnostic as the first document of every cycle.
- If the hypothesis had to be written after the activity was chosen, it is a description and not a reason to fund anything.
- If planned initiatives exceed demonstrable capacity, cut the plan before execution, not during it. Target a 1:1 plan coverage ratio; above 1.5 means fewer things finished at lower quality.
- Cap initiative count by team size: 1 marketer 2-3 a year, 2-3 marketers 3-5, 4-6 marketers 5-7, 7-10 marketers 7-10. Beyond 10, sub-functions own 3-5 each.
- If the constraint is conversion, do not spend more on top-of-funnel. Pipeline exists and is not converting.
- If the constraint is retention or expansion, top-of-funnel spend fills a leaking bucket. Put 20-30% of the plan into customer marketing.
- If the constraint is awareness, carry a 12 to 18 month lag between awareness spend and pipeline. Paid demand capture is low priority until category search volume exists.
- If the marketing leader cannot refuse requests outside the plan, there is no plan. Authority to say no is a precondition.
- Do not set quarterly milestones by dividing the annual target by four. New paid channels typically produce 40-60% of steady-state output in the first quarter; organic produces very little for two quarters then accelerates.
- Plan on median conversion rates from the last 12 months, not peak ones, and label assumptions conservative, base, or aggressive.
- Start the cycle in week 8 or 9 of Q3, not in Q4. Q4 is usually a major pipeline quarter and diverting it costs pipeline you cannot recover in Q1.

**Numbers that matter**

| Metric | Benchmark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Plan coverage ratio | 1:1 initiatives to capacity; above 1.5 is overplanning | Set at plan creation, reviewed at Q1 close |
| Hypothesis confirmation rate | 40-60%; under 30% over-ambition or weak execution; above 70% means no real bets | Annual retrospective |
| Quarterly milestone hit rate | 60-80%; under 50% over-ambitious or failing execution; above 90% unambitious | Quarterly |
| Plan-to-actual pipeline | 80-120%; under 70% poor execution or optimistic planning; above 130% may mean underplanning | Quarterly, reconciled annually |
| Strategic priority changes per year | 1-2 normal; more than 3 means reactive strategy; zero may mean ignoring the data | Annual |
| Constraint to launched initiative | Under 8 weeks at Tier 2, under 4 weeks at Tier 3+ | Over 12 weeks means an execution bottleneck the plan is not solving |
| Planning time, seed | 4-8 hours for the one-page brief, 1 hour a month | Lintel guidance |
| Planning time, Series A | 6-8 weeks, 40-60 person-hours, 2 hours a month, 2-hour quarterly replans | 10-15% of senior time during the cycle, under 5% in execution months |
| Planning time, Series B | 8-10 weeks, 80-120 person-hours across functions, 4 hours a month, half-day replans | Lintel guidance |
| Hypothesis testability | Leading-indicator signal inside 90 days | Anything not resolvable within 6 months should be split |

**Where it goes wrong:** Priorities picked from enthusiasm or competitor envy rather than a constraint diagnosis, so the real blocker stays untouched. A plan written as a document with no decision logic, so the team reverts to whatever is urgent. Flat quarterly targets that guarantee a Q1 miss. Writing only for the board, at a level of abstraction the team cannot execute against.

## The first marketing hire (cross-cutting: Section 22 of FW-FOUND-001, 005, 008, and 009)

Not a separate framework. The hiring gate each foundation framework defines, collected in one place.

**What must exist before you hire a marketing leader**
- A written first-version ICP, even rough, plus 12 to 24 months of closed-won and closed-lost data and notes on why key deals went the way they did. A leader who builds the ICP alone spends 4 to 8 weeks on it and produces their own assumptions instead of your history.
- At least one channel with 90 days of data showing qualified pipeline output.
- A revenue target they will be measured against, and a hypothesis about which channel or motion produces pipeline.
- For budget ownership: an approved annual number, a rough category allocation, and the output metric they will be held to.

**Decision rules**
- If you cannot name which customer type you win most often, this is a sales learning problem, not a marketing problem. Close 20 more deals first.
- Readiness test: you can describe your best customer in two sentences, sales agrees, and it exists in writing.
- Do not hire the first sales rep before the motion is a one-page process description with unit economics calculated.
- Full-time budget owner above $750,000 of annual marketing budget and 10+ hours a week of budget work. Fractional at $300,000 to $750,000, roughly 8-12 hours a month. Consultant for a one-time audit, cut model, or board deck, 4 to 8 weeks, maintenance owned internally.
- A fractional leader cannot own the quarterly replan or the cross-function relationships. Use fractional to set up planning infrastructure (3 to 6 months at Series A), then own it.

**Inherit vs build**
- Inherits: the ICP document, prior-year spend and channel performance, the revenue target, the GTM motion statement, the tool stack, and two weeks of founder shadowing.
- Builds: ICP validation and CRM scoring, the detailed playbook and training, the annual plan and constraint diagnostic, the efficiency framework and cut scenarios, and the quarterly replan cadence.

**30/60/90**
- 30 days: constraint diagnostic written, existing customer data reviewed, 5 to 8 customer interviews, an ICP validation memo confirming or revising the ICP with evidence. For a budget owner, the prior-year efficiency audit.
- 60 days: ICP validated, CRM scoring designed, draft annual plan with pipeline model reviewed with sales and finance.
- 90 days: scoring live, quarterly review process running, first ICP-filtered campaign launched, plan in execution with tracking in place.

**Reading candidates**
- Strong: states a constraint diagnosis from your metrics unprompted, uses hypothesis language naturally, has a stage-appropriate view on build sequencing, quotes unit economics from prior roles from memory, uses pipeline rather than lead volume as the output metric.
- Weak: answers "strategy" with a channel roadmap, cannot name the constraint the plan solves, talks about content produced and events attended, cannot name cost-per-lead benchmarks for their own prior channels, proposes a budget as a percentage of revenue with no pipeline model.
- Red flags: no view on where growth is constrained, jumps to execution before understanding the business, leads with brand investment unconnected to pipeline, defines success as growing the team, built a large sales team with no documented motion.
- Work sample: a one-page constraint diagnostic from your metrics plus two growth hypotheses, or a simplified pipeline model from a revenue target, deal size, and close rate.

**Where it goes wrong:** Hiring a senior leader with no documented hypothesis or constraint, which gives too much latitude and too little direction, so the strategy reflects their preferences. Hiring to build a sales team before the motion is documented, which produces as many motions as there are reps.

## Build order, and the two foundations that follow (FW-FOUND-006, FW-FOUND-007)

The order comes from what each framework needs as an input, stated in each source document.

1. **ICP** (FW-FOUND-001). Needs five or more paying customers. Everything downstream targets a buyer.
2. **Buyer personas** (FW-FOUND-002). Needs a validated ICP, 10+ customers, and sales no longer purely founder-led.
3. **Positioning** (FW-FOUND-003). Needs the ICP, since position is relative to a buyer and their alternatives.
4. **Messaging architecture** (FW-FOUND-004). Needs positioning, since messaging is its translation.
5. **GTM motion** (FW-FOUND-005). Needs the ICP. At pre-seed with a founder closing, document the working motion in parallel.
6. **Measurement foundation** (FW-FOUND-006). Needs the motion, since the metrics that matter depend on it. Skip under 10 customers with no material spend. First version is three numbers monthly: qualified conversations marketing produced, the share that became closed-won, and cost per qualified conversation. Agree the definitions of lead, qualified lead, and influenced pipeline before building infrastructure. That file owns funnel definitions and attribution.
7. **Budget** (FW-FOUND-008) and **planning** (FW-FOUND-009). Both need a revenue target, the motion, and whatever measurement exists.
8. **Stack architecture** (FW-FOUND-007). Follows the motion rather than leading it. Skip pre-product-market fit, and skip if the real constraint is content, positioning, or message clarity, since tools built on a messaging problem scale the problem at higher cost. First step is a CRM as the single source of truth for contacts and accounts. Vendor choice is FW-OPS-003; setup is FW-REVOPS-002.

A reasonable first-year sequence while building the function: Q1 foundations plus the primary demand channel, Q2 scale that channel, Q3 validate a second channel and start content and brand if absent, Q4 execute while the retrospective and constraint diagnostic begin in week 8 or 9 of Q3.

**Who owns what when topics collide**
- "Which way should we acquire customers?" FW-FOUND-005. "We have a sales team, how do we build and run it?" FW-SALES-001.
- "How does marketing fill the top of the motion?" FW-DEMANDGEN-001.
- Positioning problem vs wording problem: FW-FOUND-003 sets the strategy, FW-FOUND-004 writes the language.
- "Do we need this tool category?" FW-FOUND-007. "Which vendor?" FW-OPS-003. "How do we wire it up?" FW-REVOPS-002.
- Ongoing competitive intelligence as a program: FW-PM-004. Positioning and battlecards: here.
